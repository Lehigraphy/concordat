## Medic
The medic looked up from the dying soldier before his knees. Even if he hadn’t felt the thud of its footsteps in the ground under his shins, the thing blocked out what little remained of the sun. He guessed he should have been afraid. It retained just enough humanity to be recognizable as one, and just that little to be terrifying. Four limbs and a head shaped from metal and cracked black-grey plate, wielding an obscene imitation of a gun. He was looking straight into its barrel, barely a forearm’s length away. He could have fit his index finger into its rifled insides.

He felt nothing at all. If the thing wanted him dead, it would happen before he knew it, and until then he had a duty to perform. He returned to the man twitching around his forearms. Above his head, metal slid against metal and an empty case tumbled onto the ground before him. It was nothing an infantryman should fire, almost like a small repeater cannon round, bigger even than the cragguns from the distant past where forests still covered the slopes..

## Gunsmith
It began, hundreds of years ago, because a gunsmith was _bored_. She was one of the first of her trade, and she had been there in the very beginning. She hadn’t been a gunsmith yet, obviously, or even a smith for that matter. She had been a girl, sitting on her smith-mother’s lap in the hearth-forge that had been a place of wonder back then. A caravan itinerant, a woman surely older than her grandma with a wide funny hat, had brought in a weapon they had never seen before. The gunsmith had listened, eyes wide, as she held the strange rod of metal and wood over her head and spoke of an attack, of thunder and smoke and monsters in the woods, or so the gunsmith remembered it.

Later, she understood that it was wandering bandits who had attacked the caravan in a pass, just as her people understood the firearms they had carried. The shabby ambush had been beaten back and most of the outlaws killed, the few surviving youths sentenced to years in the mines, all at the cost of dented breastplates and an arm-wound. Yet, their weapons, crude as they were, could be fired by boys or widows too weak to draw a bow or swing a blade. After the workings of gunpowder were discovered – or bought or stolen or murdered for, she didn’t know – forging a barrel was almost embarrassingly easy. Soon, they were making guns of their own, and the trade of a gunsmith was born. And she knew she would be one.

And now she was bored. She sat in her workshop, alone, staring at the unattached barrel on her workbench. She, and others like her, had applied all the hard-won metalworking skills of her people on the trade. Their guns were lighter and shorter and more powerful than their predecessors. More ornamented, too, but there was only so much you could do by only working the metal. The thought of making the same gun for the rest of her life galled her. They were still single-ended tubes fixed to a wooden stock, slow and awkward to load, and the powder got wet and spilled easily.

She decided to fix this. Her guns would be loaded at least twice as fast, if not faster. She knew metal, and so she turned to metal for an answer. Powder sealed within metal would stay dry and tidy, and could be plugged into the barrel in no time at all. The barrel clattered on the floor as she swept her workbench clean, fetched a fresh piece of parchment and set to work.

Turning the notion into a working gun took thirty years of her life and required a single-minded stubbornness few possessed. It cost her a few fingers and an eye, and the opportunity to have children of her own. She barely noticed, sleeping in her workshop surrounded by half-finished attempts and wiped-out chalk drawings. She continued making the boring same muzzle-loaders, but only when her coin threatened to run out. She knew her trade and her name was known, so it was not often.

She was old when her name was finally carved on the wall over the hearth-forge and a token was made in the shape of her cartridge. It was iron instead of drawn copper, and lacked the lead ball and the wax-sealed touchhole. The gunsmith would have preferred the token to be her locking mechanism instead, but she was there when her name was carved on the wall over it. Just like she had been there all those decades ago when the old itinerant had waved the bandit’s gun over her head. Caravan guards came for her guns now, as did hunters and trappers from small villages. Her fingers were too crooked to hold a hammer any more, but she had an apprentice now to cook for her and to take over her forge when it was time. She had never expected it, and would never tell the boy as much, but she liked him. The boy’s grandfather had been a robber of some kind, he had told her once.


## Toll Kingdom

The sweating breaker was on his way to put down a pack of animals. He had two dozen others with him, and they were hauling heavy wooden shields uphill, slipping and quietly swearing on the shale. They were advancing under the cover of the night with their plate blackened with soot, their target a self-proclaimed mountain kingdom. The breaker was nervous. The _kingdom_ – was well protected by cliffs and ravines, and taking it could be bloody work. They had been pestering caravans for years, extracting tolls for a safe passage in the pass winding through the rocklands. If they had left it at that, the breaker would have rather stayed home. But he knew of the things they sometimes did, and he had a niece in the caravans. He would lose no sleep over putting the lot to the blade. 

And shot. The leather satchel on his hip was full of brass cartridges the size of his first finger, and he carried a gun on his back. Its inventor had already passed, but the breakers would now see if the gun was worth the years she’d poured into it. He had practiced loading the gun and sealing the breech with the sliding block until he could loose ten aimed shots a minute, hitting a target at sixty spans. A boy in his troop could do one hundred, thanks to his good eye and the new twisted barrel on his gun. A part of him would still have preferred the short blade on his hip. His hand went to the handle in response.

In the end, the battle hardly earned its name. They advanced quietly behind their shields, four men apiece, firesticks glowing red dots in the dark. Drunken bellows from the settlement covered the rasp of their plate and the occasional clatter of a kicked stone. For a time, it seemed like they had brought the shields for nothing and it would come to blades after all. The breaker tensed in anticipation of the charge and the crash. Then, at sixty spans, the master called the attack.

Two dozen guns boomed in unison. The sound was deafening, but no worse than the new watermill hammer at his brother’s forge. The gun kicked his breastplate like a brute, but he had braced for it and only rocked back and forth again. Ahead, he could faintly see chips fly in the darkness as their bullets hit the timber wall. Immediately before him, the two breakers had already shouldered their guns and were straining to lift the shield a few spans forward. He was already breeching a new round. After it was spent, he took his turn at the shield, and on, two or three spans at a time.

Bodies littered the rocky ground when it was over, only a few minutes later. The smoke burned in the breaker’s throat as he stood in the  ruin of shards that had been the wall. Some of the brigands had valiantly died on it, but most had simply run until they remembered there was nowhere to run in the ravine. They were on their knees now. Fools. They would find little mercy in front of the magistrates. A life in the mines or the barges was the best they might wish for.



## Logger

A century or so after the emplacement, a logger overseer visited the workshop of a low-valley gunsmith on his trip to fetch salt and supplies. They had felled the last tree of the grove, so in a fortnight his workband would be moving further away from the banks of Northern through unmapped thickets and gullies. He could use a gun or two more. The smith listened to the logger’s story, nodding, and told the logger he had exactly what he needed. He bent under his desk and lifted a shiny new gun on it, and a cartridge next to it.

The logger’s lip curled. He had come with the best intentions, but the smith was obviously jesting with him. The round was too short by a finger’s width and besides it had…shoulders. A tiny bullet sat on it, curved and ending in a blunt tip like the helms that the traders from the north sometimes wore. His wife might carry that on her neck, aye, but he needed something powerful against beasts. They roamed the forests, for meat and coin.

The smith explained. They had better powder now, much more powerful, so the old fifteen-and-half mil bore had been reduced to thirteen, and the case given a neck to fit it. Any larger and it would kick a man flat. The logger wasn’t convinced until the smith took him to the fields to try it. It kicked. Stronger than his old gun, nearly making him stumble, and the crack hurt his ears. He bought four. He would have to let his master fire a shot before he let him know how much they had cost. 

They were a day away from their logging camp, worming their way between bushes and saplings, when the overseer froze, lifting his fist for his band to do likewise. Something had cracked. Something thick, a fallen trunk or a root perhaps. His eyes scanned the dense underbrush for what had caught his attention. His hand crept towards the leather strap holding his rifle. A bead of sweat slid down his temple.

A deep bellow shook his bowels. A heartbeat later, a dark shape barged through the thicket. Arm-thick saplings snapped as it charged, head down, huffing like the biggest bellows. The rifle strap almost slipped from the logger’s fumbling fingers. Male curlhorns were vicious creatures with thick skin and thicker fur, and this was the biggest he had seen by far. For an instant, he wished to hold his sons one more time. But he was dead. They were all dead. They could fire all their guns at the brown-haired boulder, and it would still trample them to mush before dying from its wounds. He shouldered the rifle and cocked the hammer all the same.

It was over before the logger knew it. The flesh on his upper chest ached where the gun had kicked into it. He still held it against his shoulder, arms suddenly shaking, fingers squeezed white against the wooden stock. One of his band lay still in a trampled heap on the path that the curlhorn had ploughed into undergrowth. The animal itself was on its stomach, moss piled against its snout. It shuddered once and then lay still, and the logger hesitantly lowered his rifle. He swallowed to clear his throat, and nodded once to the animal, out of respect and as thanks for its meat and skin.

## Cook

_Give me Thirteen and a pair-o good hands._

The cook grimaced, and then broke into a chuckle and shook her head. What the guards lacked in tune they certainly made up in sheer loudness. The men were enthusiastic, and clearly having a good time. As far as she could tell, they hadn’t even had a drop of moonshine, only a small cup of beer each. One was plucking at the six strings of a wooden instrument, while another tapped on the wooden stock of his gun for beat. The metal parts were covered in tiny engravings, she noticed only now. Maybe they were past owners, or marks of caravans seen safely through.

_We stand fast and we let ‘em come._

They weren’t their regular guards, these three. They wore plain jackets instead of the embroidered wraps that her own people favoured, and carried identical guns that didn’t seem to need a waxcord or a firestick to work. The caravan leader had hired their guns when they passed through the foundry valleys, along with six crates of guns. She had expected the guard leader would have been slighted at that. Instead, the man had nodded approvingly at the news, and later shown the newcomers around himself. She had been helping to load the crates on wagon two at the time. The Two Ravines Pass was full of perils, he said, so better to have as many gun-hands as possible.

_The wagons roll on while one of us stands._

They were through the pass now, but there had been no singing in the six nights before. The foundry guards had disappeared into the maze of needlebush and knife-sharp splitrock every time they stopped for the day. Once, a scrape or a clatter had woken her up in the dead of the night. Rivets had pressed against her forehead as she peered through the armored viewing slit, ready to yell an alarm, hand on her knife already. It had been just one of the new guards, tiptoeing back to the wagons. It must have been well past midnight, judging from how the fire had gone out almost entirely, only a few embers smouldering in the ring of rocks. The man had been wiping a nasty-looking blade with a piece of cloth, his gun on the crook of his arm. His eyes had chilled her. They had gone as cold as the tools his people loved to trade.

_We stand fast and we let ‘em come._

Now, the same eyes were laughing again. He was working an oiled cloth through the barrel of his gun, and bellowing what she hoped were the last verses of the song. The other two had their rifles on their laps. They never cleaned them at the same time, she had noticed. Idly, she wondered if the guns they had bought for trading were the same, or if the foundries reserved the best for their own. They certainly weren’t engraved, she had seen that much, yet had still cost more coin than she had ever seen in one place.

She let out a sigh of relief when the song ended, only to bite it back as the guards started butchering the opening verses of the Forgemaster’s Son. This she had at least learned the words to. If she joined the song, the men might follow her and actually hit the tune this time. One could always hope.

## Cartographer

The cartographer slumped down, gasping. Her fingers were raw, every muscle of her body ached, and the rifle on her back dug painfully into her spine as it caught between her and the boulder. Scaling the mesa had taken all morning and nearly all her strength. She allowed herself to lay still until her breath came a little easier, before easing the leather rifle strap off her shoulder. It came off easily, but her entire side convulsed before she got the rifle from behind her back and on the rock next to her. One did not travel the badlands without a gun ready. Not for long. She knew she should also spread her drenched jacket out to dry, and mine out the thick overcoat from her bag before the wind stole the heat from her body.

That would wait, however. She couldn’t wait to check the vista against her drawings and calculations. She knew the rocklands were passable. Knew it in her bones. When she found the way through the cracks and ravines, it could cut the journey from Northern to Tinstream in half. Perhaps down to a third even, and it would let them avoid the Ravines altogether.

Her eyes passed over the gun as she bent over to her satchel for her notebook. The stock had received a new scuff, adding to those already crisscrossing it. She tutted at the sight. The rifle had passed down in her family since it saved her great-grandfather and his work party from a rampaging grazer. Well, she had replaced the old scratchlock with the new percussion mechanism, but the barrel still fired the reliable, crushing thirteen. Her father and grandmother had carved their marks on the barrel and the receiver. So far, she had only added dents and scuffs.

With the notebook in her other hand, she hauled out a looking glass hanging from her neck. The decrepit settlement visible in the distance, some hundreds of spans lower than she, provided a handy point of reference. She had seen the shattered walls and collapsed roofs up close when she had passed it two days ago. Some kind of a battle had obviously been fought there in the past.

She turned her glass a quarter circle north and began scribbling measurements on her notebook. There was a promising depression snaking its way between peaks, perhaps a day’s journey away. Frost started forming on the shell of her coat, unnoticed.

## Guard

The guard master rolled his shoulders and shifted his feet in the open turret. Below him, the chest-high wheels ground against the compacted gravel. The caravan was almost through the Tinstream Depression, but a crew who thought they were in the clear and let their guard down was easy prey for an ambush. And there had been lots of ambushes recently. The caravans were running day and night to keep the furnaces running, and he was sure at least a few of the dozen valley-states surrounding them were sanctioning privateers to profit from it. Not four hours ago, they had passed the gutted remains of a Saltswale convoy.

Well, they wouldn’t find such easy pickings in us, the master thought and spat downwind. Behind him, a valve clicked and a shrill hiss ensued. The tank on his back was drawing new pressure from the hose tethering him to the steel-plated truck. The rig he was wearing reduced the heavy repeating mountain rifle in his hands to little more than a regular carbine. The old reliable thirteen-mil slugs would make short work of the hastily dug ambush nests that had surrounded the destroyed vehicles earlier. 

Still, he muttered a prayer to the old saints for an uneventful passage. He had two first-timers with him, so fresh it hurt. The boy in the sponson below him was barely rigged, for the patron’s sake. They had left his fitters, an apprentice as young as the boy and a master, at the last waystation before the Depression. The boy could surely shoot his gun from the turret just fine, but if they needed to kick open their armored doors and fan out, he wasn’t so sure. The modified disaster relief rigs around them were strong and he very much preferred his steel chestplate taking the oxgun’s formidable recoil, but their untethered endurance was abysmal. And while the anti-crush plates might ricochet a glancing hit, they were no truck armor.  

That might change, though, the guard master thought while his eyes kept examining the slopes ahead for fresh signs of digging or turned stones. He wasn’t supposed to know anything about it, but the caravaniers spoke like nobody else. The compassman’s cousin’s friend’s partner, or something, designed tiles for kilns and had been tiring everybody within earshot to death with uninvited lectures on crystal structures and whatnot. But now, he worked with the Hammer and was keeping himself quiet with obvious effort. That was telling enough.

## Loader

The journeyman of loading stamped his seal on the wooden box and wiped his brow. That was the last one, he thought with relief, and he didn’t even have to argue with the bald grid-counter about overtime. He nodded at the men and women hovering expectantly around him, a signal to start loading the boxes into the awaiting six-wheeler, stenciled for Tinstream. Its driver was leaning against the gateframe further away, staring into the distance with dark rings under his eyes.

Another nod, and a chime signalled the end of work for the week. He only had to do his round of triple-checks, and then he’d have plenty of time to catch the evening-bell train to Oldmill. The bottle of imported sparkling wine should be thoroughly chilled by now. His partner – informal for now, but he had plans for that – had mentioned she liked that particular design of grapes or something, and the master of inventory at the bottle store had handled the rest.

The charging station was the first on his list. The apprentices had done an impeccable job of zeroing the scales and ensuring that not a speck of powder remained around the shaker. He knew they had – he himself had hammered its importance on them – but he still checked every corner and bolt. Nobody wanted to repeat the disaster at Southern.

The seating and crimping tables were just as fine, but nothing could be done for the dangerously low stockpile of empty cases. Well, there was nothing that he could do, he shrugged. People like her soon-to-be promised were working their numbers on the copper supply, but he understood it might be up to a year before he saw the effects.

Once, after a nice supper and a few glasses of wine, she’d fumed for an hour how his job made hers so much harder. Each of his cases had enough brass for three to six rounds for Anvil riflemen, she had demonstrated with her fingers. He had tried echoing his master’s words on how the Hammer needed more punch and bringing the famous thirteen’s black powder predecessor up to today’s metals provided just that punch, but his argument had faded into mumbling halfway through at the sight of her expression. Luckily, the waiter had arrived at that very point before he embarrassed himself entirely .

The cartridge recycling program should begin to help soon, however. For a month, he had marked reclamation quotas on the boxes, and the first empty cases should begin trickling back perhaps as early as next week. Incorporating them into the loading process was his responsibility, too. Inspection checklists and cleaning rotations still filled his mind as the ammunition truck rumbled away. He almost didn’t notice the jagged holes that spattered its flank.

## Medic

The medic climbed back on his feet, wiping his hands to the best of his abilities with the last of his sanitizer. The thing had moved on, and the soldier at his feet had died. Just as the medic had known he would, even before he started. He would have died even in a surgery room full of doctors and buckets of banked blood. Half of his chest cavity had burst open, organs pulped by the pressure. The medic had still needed to try.

He shuffled off to find somewhere and someone to try in vain again. He wouldn’t have to look long.

It defied understanding. Order had collapsed entirely, and he had been reduced to running from wounded to wounded like some war hero in a film whose makers had never seen battle. He guessed it couldn’t have been more than a quarter, half-hour at most, but it felt like hours. Hours of head-sized exit wounds full of jellied tissue, of faces no longer recognizable as faces let alone the person who once wore them. Two decades of petty ambushes and squabbles over tributaries had hardened him against screaming. Here, almost nobody did and somehow that was much worse. The few who did, he could at least try to help, wrapping compression bandages around limbs nearly shorn off.

The medic found another. A single glimpse at the uniformed heap told him he wouldn’t need bandages here. Coming from somewhere behind him, a series of concussive hammerblows shook the ruined walls around him.

The medic knelt again.
