He was Marek, a mill-worker. He sat in the local hole, the back of his stool against the wall, and sipped at his drink. Opposite him, Lennisson gestured with his half-empty pint. A dash of beer spilled on the table, joining the slowly drying sticky pool already there. Lennisson had run out of his theses half an hour ago, so now he was going through them again.

‘We should lob the head off that ox-drop governor. Like in the old days. A piece of ox-drop, that one.’

‘You got that one right, but maybe keep it down a little.’ Marek put on his embarrassed face and clinked his pint against Lennisson’s. It was still three-quarters full.

Marek was the sympathetic but responsible co-worker. One that let Lennisson vent but also saw that he made it home afterward without getting into too much trouble. Maybe covered for him the next morning, too, for a few hours at least.

He knew Lennisson’s type. He would join the mob when things got properly underway, and be among the first to cheer when it was over, but for bridging the gap between then and now, he was a liability.

But he always attracted the right kind of attention. Everybody knew where he stood, and that standing was all he would do. Marek craned his neck to check the clock on the wall and quickly scanned the crowd there while turning back.

There was a group of dockworkers hunched over their pints, conversing quietly and glancing around themselves every few moments. Marek knew their type, too. Later.

There. A woman sitting by herself. Her face sullen, an untouched glass on the table. But underneath, she was alive. Anger, but controlled. Looking for a way out.

Marek caught her eye.

He was Ilan, an assistant to master Venn. He stood a respectful distance from the Lord Baron’s table, his back against the wall. The old man held the pen Ilan had just presented him before retreating with a bow.

‘You have managed to convince me, master Venn. The gods above and below both know that is not a straightforward task.’ His moustache curled with his smile.

‘I know you have the best of your people in your mind, Lord Baron,’ the master replied.

Ilan believed so, too, but the baron’s idea of what was best for his people ‌had differed from theirs. Master Venn had worked tirelessly to make him see the reason behind the trade deal, and Ilan had worked even more tirelessly to deliver the numbers to support that reason. Among other things.

The baron signed, an elaborate tangle of arcs and curls. The scraping of his pen on the paper was the only sound in the room.

Then it was done. The baron lowered the pen. His courtiers clapped, as did the master’s staff. At a nod from Venn, Ilan excused himself to fetch a bottle of Naentyr’s, matured for thirty years. It was the baron’s favourite, a fact Ilan had pulled from his housemaster weeks ago.

He would drop the small, unlabeled vial of insulin into a trashcan on his way to the banquet. No need for that now, or an ultrathin needle between the toes. With a little luck, the baron’s grandson would have to wait for his succession for ten more years. Plenty of time for him to finish his studies in Southern. Maybe even find a partner, though Ilan of course had no contact at all with that part of the operation.

He popped open the Naentyr.

He was Tomas, a courier. He walked along the alley, the decrepit brick wall brushing against his shoulder, and kicked over a loose stone. It was almost midnight, raining and chilly. The gas lights flickered, those that had deigned to stay lit at all.

Tomas wore his nondescript coat of worn leather, and his nondescript face. His cap was low on his brow to protect against the freezing rain, and he had pushed his dispatch bag under the coat’s hem to keep it dry. Half a dozen telegrams in it, and a few parcels. His shoe made a metallic sound as it scraped against the wall.

He stopped at an intersection to consult the street signs and to wipe the water from his brow. The night watchman at the plant would get his telegram next, a short distance to the right. Tomas would then double back along the merchants’ lane. Hopefully, the awnings had been left open and would offer a little cover from the rain.

Tomas dug a wad from his mouth and squished it against the wall, before setting off towards the plant. To a casual observer, were anybody actually observing anything in this weather, it would look like nothing more than an unsanitary disposal of chewing leaves.

Behind Tomas, another light died as its valve was shut off. He never looked at the shadows moving forward.

***

He was home. He sat in the cafe, his chair pushed against the wall. The owner had changed since the last time, and the selection on the green-tinted glass shelves was new. The coffee was as bad as ever, so at least that one aspect was familiar.

He stirred his spoon in the cup, idly, or so anybody would think, and looked through the window. A tram passed. It had appeared when he was home ‌last time. Or perhaps the one before that. It was getting hard to remember.

It was getting dark outside, and the glass reflected his face. Grey was now the dominant colour in his hair. His real hair.

He craned his neck to check the clock on the wall and casually scanned the other customers while turning back. A shift of glassworkers hunched over their cups. They were tired. Grumbling.

He knew their type.
