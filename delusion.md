The Count had started raving today. It sounded like a simple occurrence in his line of work, yet this one resisted putting itself in a report. For the third time, he erased all he had written. He hammered at the key, then chided himself for doing so. Requisitioning a new terminal would take months. Outside his sole window, a cloud moved across the moon and the shadows in his room shifted. He tried and failed to not think about iron moving in the dark. A shiver rippled down his spine. Was it cold here, or hot?

It was…impossible, yet the Count had screamed it in front of his very eyes, veins bulging as four wardens struggled to restrain him. The old man’s sickness had always been so benign, even if it was stubborn. The doctor would have sent him home decades ago, if not for his stubborn conviction that his _courtiers_ take care of the boring practicalities like clothing and feeding him. Yet the things he rambled about today had genuinely frightened him.

Particular delusions spreading between his patients wasn’t uncommon. They spoke, most of them, or hollered at the padded walls of their rooms. Others heard, and a mind already damaged could easily take impressions. The last month in the closed yard was unprecedented though, both in the number of patients affected and the eerie similarities in their babbling. For the first time in his career, the doctor had had nightmares about his work. He had woken up drenched in sweat, the scrape of metal on stone still in his ears.

But they absolutely shouldn’t spread from the closed yard to the open like this. The cases never met each other, and the yards were physically separated by spans of centuries-old stone. He had nearly dropped his slate when the Count started rambling about black iron in a room of death and smoke, too. The same hallucination that had been spreading like a contagion, down to the details of broken marble covered in glass and the particular taste of the smoke in the air. The old man had become so agitated, pulling at his hair and twisting around in his chair, that the doctor had no other choice than to put him under before his heart gave out.

‘It has her face,’ the man had muttered piteously as his eyelids slid shut. That was a new detail, but it didn’t really make it any better.

It had to be one of the kitchen staff speaking out of turn, or maybe one of the apprentice wardens. He hoped it was, because the other option was that the night wardens had been moving the patients around for some despicable betting ring or something. That went far beyond incompetence and petty malpractice. He would bring the magistrates down on them hard if he found out.

And he did intend to find out. It was why he had stayed late in his locked office, the single light switched off. He would sneak out soon, to some textile closet or a drug cabin, and listen. He should have been able to use the cameras for that, but the signal router still hadn’t been replaced.

Soon, it was late enough. The corridor outside would be empty. He stopped once to press his ear against the door, and then slid into the hallway. He had just closed his door again when the dim night lights went off again. The doctor froze. It was almost tar-black. Where had the damned moon gone? His heart began to pound. It didn’t feel like the main hallway. He thought he could see…paintings on the walls. That couldn’t be right. Frames weren’t safe. His hair rose, and he tried to ignore the all too real taste at the back of his tongue. It was just the dark, and the phenomenon with the patients. Just a cloud across the moon. Just as he had almost convinced himself, he took a step and immediately wished he had not.

Under his feet, glass crunched.
