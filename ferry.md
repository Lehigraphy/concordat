‘Morning, master. Three tickets please. One regular, two return.’

‘I ain’t a master, but thank you, young sire. That’ll be fifteen and thirty-five.’

Rovan counted the coins and lowered them onto the counter of the ticket booth. Or perhaps an enclosure was the better term. A few planks forming a frame with no actual walls, let alone a roof, and a hand-painted plaque of the current fares. The moustached man inside took them all the same and handed Rovan a triplet of paper tickets in return, tipping the threadbare cap on his head as a thank you.

‘There you go. That friend of yours looks like he should stay at the railing. Easier on your stomach if you look at the banks, you know. And you won’t have to clean the deck if…you know.’

Rovan thanked the man and turned towards the other two. Onvek didn’t look that bad in his opinion. True, his uniform could have used ironing. And some time in fresh air, for that matter. But it still had all the pieces as far as he could tell, down to ceremonial gloves extending halfway up his forearms.

‘Thank you,’ Onvek said as Rovan passed him his ticket. ‘Wenzel you’ve officially saved my skin. I’ll pay you back on my next leave.’

‘Maybe you’ll also deign to settle your debts to myself while you’re at it. You owe me three ales, and for that time at the eatery for that matter, if I reckon right.’

Rovan couldn’t help but ‌chuckle. Wenzel’s affront may have been mocked, but his manner of speech was all natural. It was peculiar, really. Wenzel had been five when his valley was aligned and his family moved inwards, but he’d somehow managed to retain the aristocratic patterns of his first language, down to the nasal tone.

‘Do the Anvil not pay your fares any more? All’s going to the breachers, aye?’

‘They do, yes, but–’

‘–but you blew it all on drinks after you disappeared last night.’ Rovan completed the sentence. Onvek smiled and shrugged, palms up in a helpless gesture.

‘Hey, hey! There were girls, too!’ He seemed to consider a moment, pinching the bridge of his nose. ‘I think.’

Rovan exchanged a knowing glance with Wenzel. For a moment, all three just stood on the pier, looking at the fog-covered river. It was an uncharacteristically still morning, allowing the vapour to concentrate and turning the surface of the water into a mirror. Visibility was down to two dozen spans, so all it reflected was the ferry. It could have used some rust remover and a fresh layer of paint.

‘I don’t mind, though. You know.’ Onvek’s voice was quiet. His expression had turned serious.

‘The Hammer getting all the expensive gear. The breachers go first and wreck everything. Nothing left to bother us.’

Again, nobody spoke until Rovan cleared his throat.

‘How is it?’

‘Quite boring, actually. We ride in, run like rabbits to get the crusher set up.’ Now, Onvek chuckled. ‘Then sit behind it for hours drinking coffee. Then run again once the wheelers arrive.’

‘But that’s actually quite interesting. Everyone’s an engineer. Hey, did I tell you already? Last month we got to blow up something. There was this really old bridge that was so decrepit that–’

The chime of a bell interrupted Onvek. It was a single ding only, but enough to carry in the near-silence around them. The ferry driver, who turned out to also be their cashier, was signaling that it was time to board.

***

They were the only ones on the ferry, apart from the driver of course. The driver ripped a corner off Onvek’s ticket but shook his head when Wenzel offered him his. They’d need them for the trip back, he said. The engine spluttered into life and coughed out a few clouds of oily smoke, shaking the deck, but then started properly and settled into a rhythmic chug-chug. The entire vessel swayed as they set off. For a moment, Onvek did look decidedly uncomfortable. Then the motion died, apart from their slow but steady pace forward, evident only from the clouds of fog drifting past them. The deck thrummed gently beneath their feet. Soon, the fog swallowed the bank, and there was nothing to see.

‘We ought to do this more often, do you concur?’ Wenzel said after a while. 

Rovan didn’t disagree. The week had gone by entirely too fast.

‘In-spec,’ he said. ‘I’ll be busy with ‘prentice for the next two months or so, but I’ll walk if they won’t give me a day or two. When’s your next leave, Onvek?’

‘We’re moving out, so don’t know yet. But I’ll send you a message when I find out. Maybe we could go to the Slope again?’

‘We won’t fit in the sled any more.’

‘Let’s purchase a second one. You can race, and I can aid when you crash.’

Rovan laughed and shook his head. When he looked up again, he saw an empty pier appear gradually from the fog. The rest of the riverbank followed a moment later.

‘Hey. Before I forget,’ he said and dug out a few coins from his pocket. Wenzel took a moment to catch up, then reached for a purse himself, too. ‘Here. Buy yourself a coffee and a roll.’

Onvek shook his head, hands spread in refusal.

‘I can afford a coffee myself.’

‘You can most definitely not afford a coffee. Nor a roll,’ Wenzel said.

Onvek sighed and then extended his hand in acceptance.

‘Thank you. Again.’

The engine died and the ferry bumped into the pier a few ticks later. The driver jumped down with a rope and tied it onto a bollard. Onvek looked at both Rovan and Wenzel in turn.

‘Guess this is it. My transport leaves in…seven,’ he said after consulting his wristwatch.

Wenzel reached out to shake Onvek’s hand, his face solemn. 

‘Your hearth stay warm,’ he said.

Rovan waited for them to finish and then stepped forward to hug Onvek. Arms on upper back, a few hearty slaps.

‘Stay safe there.’

‘Will do. But hey, got to move now.’ Onvek picked up his backpack and climbed onto the pier. The driver was walking back towards the ferry, probably having checked that there was nobody waiting further away on the bank. He was already unmooring the ferry when Onvek reached the bollard and thanked him for the ride.

The fog had almost swallowed him when Wenzel suddenly called after him.

‘Where are you transferring? You might get a ride back on one of my uncle’s transports. Save your coin for drink again.’

Onvek turned around and spread his arms wide, walking backwards just as the ferry’s engine coughed itself back to life again.

‘Some place called Weiter. That’s all I know.’

The ferry swayed into motion again. It was just the two of them now, and the ferryman.
