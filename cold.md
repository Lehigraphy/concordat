# Dump

Talvek jumped up to escape the burning pain on his lap, only to bang his knee against the control panel. The clatter of his mug on the floor was barely audible over the clamor. The furnace was roaring loud enough to hurt his ears, louder than he had ever heard it. Under the soles of his boots, the floor vibrated. Somewhere inside the control panel, a loose bolt rattled. Over it all, the warning whistle was issuing its shrill wail, not that he needed it to know something was wrong.

Talvek reached down to massage the joint, cursing. Around him, the control chamber was a box of near darkness. The only genuine source of light was the yellow-white glow from the feed channels of the furnace. It should have been tinted red. Another indication that something was terribly wrong. The furnace stood behind the window, a building-sized shape of black iron and tiles reaching down into the darkness for more than a dozen spans from where he stood. It felt suddenly threatening, its outline distorted by ripples in the decades-old glass.

Then he realized what must have happened. The low hum of the vibrating electrodes and bus bars had filled his shifts ever since his first job–apprenticeship–in the recycling yard, and he had learned to know the mood of the furnace from its strength and tone. He had never heard it this hungry, though. A glance at the control panel confirmed his fears. The lights and most of the dials were dead, concurrent with the low-voltage system having failed. Only those taking their readings by other means still worked. The needle for the main current was all the way to the right. 

Talvek swallowed. It wasn’t supposed to go like this. He’d followed every procedure and made sure every reading remained optimal, adjusting a dial here or a switch there if they didn’t. And before, he’d poured his all into learning his trade. Yet, something clearly had broken.

The color of the light on the panel changed. Talvek turned to find Kador walking through the doorway, the zipper of his brown overalls half-open. He moved urgently, his usual limp barely there. He was holding a portable light in his hand, pointing it sideways at the back wall so as not to blind Talvek with the glare. The light reflected from the sheet metal, polished by countless brushes of hands and backs of overalls, and cast his face in a half-illuminated relief. The wrinkles crossing his skin looked like black ravines.

‘Broke the entire foundry now, boy?’ Kador shouted over the noise. Talvek couldn’t help but chuckle a little. ‘Boy’ was how Kador had first greeted him over ten years ago. He had just arrived at the foundry, and in Oerfil, asking for work with nothing but a weird dialect and the charcoal-stained clothes on his back. Years later, when Talvek completed his third apprenticeship, Kador had slipped him a bottle of moonshine and then seemed vaguely disappointed when he showed up to work on time the next morning. But he was still a ‘boy’. Before Talvek could answer, the older man continued.

‘The valley’s got power, but the entire slaggin’ foundry’s gone dark.’ He gestured with his head toward the hallway behind him. Its paned windows looked over the roofs of the town, shingles and sheet metal both, and at night you could see its lights reach all the way to the river. Then his gaze moved to the control panel. Talvek thought the good eye widened a fraction, while the milky one remained unseeing. Kador coughed a few times to clear his throat and swallowed.

'Controls are out too,’ Talvek stated aloud what the older man must have worked out already. Kador nodded knowingly. Then he walked over to the control panel and shone his light on it. Talvek turned to look, too.

It was worse than he had feared. The main needle remained stubbornly in the red. The temperature gauge remained dead, but Talvek thought the glare from the furnace had already turned brighter and obtained a bluish tint. But it was the pressure bubble for the main hydraulic circuit that made his blood run cold. It was almost a third of the way down and sinking before his eyes. Kador must have seen his reaction.

‘Still haven’t found the leak,’ he shrugged as a half-apology. ‘There’s always something to break down, no matter how fast you go fixing.’ For a moment, both stood unspeaking. Talvek desperately hoped that the old man would take it from here. That he would say something, tell him what to do. He did not. 

‘You think—’ he tried. Kador shook his head, smiling sadly.

‘Don’t matter what I think, boy. Barely a first ‘prentice, myself. It’s you who’s got the brains here.’ Talvek had known that would be his answer.

The decision was his. And so would be the consequences, whatever he chose. He could let the furnace be, send runners for the master electricians and engineers, and hope they could restore the power and controls in time. If they did, and if nothing had been too badly damaged, they would simply fall behind their targets for the day. Even with some repairs to be taken, they could catch up in time for the next levy.

If. The uncertainty made Talvek swallow. If they didn’t regain control, they would be utterly, thoroughly slagged. Presently, the arc was drawing all the power that the grid could deliver. Before long, it would melt the protective lining covering the inside of the furnace and then crack the outer shell itself. The melt would pour out onto the foundry floor, and the entire furnace would have to be rebuilt. They’d need to tear off half of the foundry just to build the cranes needed to lift the thousand-ton mass away. That alone would be months of cold time, and never mind how long it would take to get a new one built. 

The pressure of Kador’s hand on his chest raised him from his thoughts. He had begun pacing back and forth in front of the panel at some point. Talvek turned to the foundryhand.

‘The entire foundry, you say?’ 

Kador simply nodded.

‘Think I saw shards of glass on the floor, too. From the tubes, I reckon.’ 

The indecision was a physical force tearing Talvek apart. The pressure bubble was two-thirds down already. His other option was to dump the furnace, to use the emergency hydraulics to physically tilt the entire mass upside down. Tapping the furnace like that was part of regular operation in some foundries, but Oerfil’s operated on a continuous principle, with the liquid steel drained via tapping spouts instead. Even worse, the furnace had been upgraded some decades ago, its capacity increased by over a half. The pit below that was supposed to catch the white-hot spill of metal had not. An emergency tap would flood the foundry floor with over eight hundred tons of white-hot liquid. It might cut Talvek and Kador’s only way out, and scorch anyone unlucky enough to get in its way.

And the furnace would go cold. A shudder went through him, cold as glacial ice. The furnace had been a part of his life for a decade now. It had _been_ his life, its warmth always there, predictably the same every day. And now it might go cold–by his hand. That just didn’t happen, he knew now. Furnaces only went cold when valleys did, or a plague broke out. It would take weeks of back-breaking labor to clean the solidified slag and steel off, and it could take years before they made up for the delay. If they ever made up for it, he bit his lip. But it would still be routine compared to rebuilding the entire furnace. The overall was clammy against his upper back. His hand opened the protective lid covering the emergency lever. 

He needed to decide soon. He could only dump it while the circuit had pressure, and it was bleeding out terrifyingly fast. Even the warning whistle had grown lower in tone. He would have given anything for someone else to call it. If it had happened just a few hours later, the decision would have been the foundry master's, and he would have been asleep in his apartment.

The bubble was almost down. Talvek squeezed his eyes shut, so tight it hurt, and pushed his fingertips against his forehead. Colors danced in his eyes, around an image of a furnace frozen solid. He opened his eyes. The control chamber seemed to sway around him. His vision narrowed to the control lever and to his hand fumbling at it. He didn’t feel its knurled metal surface against his skin.

He pushed.

Talvek felt more than heard a deep, thudding boom. The main breaker opening in its vault. A low rumble began to shake the chair beneath him. His eyes remained fixed on the lever, unseeing. Then he was running down the stairs. Kador had physically hauled him from the panel and into the stairwell. His overalls were still bunched on his left shoulder where he’d grabbed it. In the corner of his eye, the dark shape of the furnace loomed. It was pitching over slowly, so very slowly, but Talvek knew it would only pick up speed. The warning whistle had been replaced by the ear-bursting wail of a siren, drowning every other sound beneath itself. The beam of Kador’s light flickered over the hall. A glimpse of workers running for the doors. The edges of the perforated metal stairs under his feet catching the light. He wondered if they would collapse under the coming avalanche of heat.

They were still a level from the ground when the staircase shook and a blindingly bright white glow lit the hall. Blistering heat burned the side of his face. The heat was pouring out of the furnace. Talvek began taking three stairs at a time.

He reached the floor. Molten steel was now pouring out of the furnace in a solid stream, thick as a tree trunk and retina-searing hot. Talvek lifted his hand to shield against the glare, squinting his eyes and peeking through the gaps between his fingers.

As he watched, the surface of the liquid metal spill reached the edge of the pit and then crept over it. The concrete floor began to crackle and pop, sending white-hot sparks flying into the air. The residual water inside the concrete flashing to steam, Talvek remembered from a safety manual he had memorized. 

He was just about to move for the doors when the floor exploded with an earsplitting crack. Thousands of flying droplets burst into the air, each eye-searingly bright, sending pain needling into Talvek’s eyes. A flying chunk of concrete smashed into his knee with a nasty crack. Talvek flinched away from the sight and hunched over, covering his eyes with a forearm and clutching his knee with his other hand. Crisscrossing afterimages obscured his vision.

It took precious ticks, but finally the world returned, in vague shapes slowly resolving into a sight from a nightmare. Workers lay strewn on the floor, amidst puddles of fire. Some were stirring and beginning to pick themselves up. Too many were not. Somebody was on their knees and frantically patting out the flames on a shape writhing on the floor. Talvek thought he could hear screams over the siren and the roar and the cracks.

He was about to rush forward to help them when a hand gripped his arm. Kador leaned towards his ear. 

‘I have to go to secure the gas main.’ Despite having to yell over the siren, the old foundryhand's voice sounded disturbingly calm, the words rolling out slow and clear. Talvek thought he heard a weird timbre color it. Before he could say anything, Kador moved his hand on Talvek’s shoulder, the fingers squeezing tight like a vice.

‘I’ll join you later, son.’

Kador gripped his other arm, turned him forcefully around, and pushed him forward. Talvek stumbled onto his hurt knee and then looked over his shoulder, only to see Kador making his way towards the back of the hall. Under the furnace, the spill was creeping forward terrifyingly fast, painful to look at. The air felt like it were on fire.

He took one more look back, only to see Kador disappear behind a conveyor, and then at the hall again. Workers were limping towards the doors or dragging those who were unconscious or worse. With a groan of effort, he climbed back onto his feet and took a step. Pain lanced into his knee, but he barely noticed it. The second step came easier. Then the third and the fourth, limping towards the doors, waving and shouting the workers on. Around him, the air turned from scorching to suffocating. White-hot heat seared his face. 

Talvek barely felt any of it.

# Interlude

It all began with a small, inconspicuous component. A finger-thick cylinder of glass between caps of brass, sitting with a dozen of its friends inside a metal cabin. But within itself, it hid a microscopic flaw. Nobody could tell by just looking at it. For years, it had functioned exactly as it was supposed to. It was a vacuum tube, and it was ‌part of the control circuit of an 800-ton arc furnace in the valley of Oerfil.

Of course, vacuum tubes had been near-obsolete for more than half a century. The control circuit had been awaiting renewal for almost as long. But steel was always sorely needed for bridges and railways and vehicles, and Oerfil did not match the raw capacity of the foundries lining the banks of Central upstream of Foundry Prime, it still was among the three dozen biggest producers of it. The Concordat could ill afford shutting it down for the renewal. Thus, masters of glass kept blowing new tubes, and the furnace was kept lit.

But the tube was faulty. The glassmaster had done an impeccable job with what he had, applying all of his six decades of experience. Unfortunately, the entire glass industry had been battling supply issues for almost a decade at the time. The highest-quality silica deposits had been all but exhausted, their supply earmarked for high-end uses like precision instruments and glassware for those with the coin for it. Those lower on the priority line made do with what they got.

Like the beer jugs in common taverns or the windows in temporary barracks, the vacuum tube was tinted green. This time, the iron impurities—iron that the Concordat so loved—in the silica had also caused a microscopic defect in the crystal structure of the glass. It was too small to be detected in the initial inspection and only grew at a glacial pace when the furnace ran. The people of the foundry knew their trade, and the demands on the control circuit were gentle. Had the situation stayed normal, it would have lasted wel beyond its designated overhaul.

Then, a bomb went off in Karsik.

It was of subpar make, and most of the fertilizer failed to detonate. The explosion simply sent it flying around, barely scorching the stone wall of the transformer station. But it did shake the building enough for a protective breaker to trip for more than ten ticks.

Karsik was a major exporter of electric power, and the sudden absence of that power caused a voltage dip measured thousands of kilo spans away. Inside the furnace, the arc writhed and shrank. The current sagged by nearly two-tenths, and the resulting force transient on the feed bars shook the control cabinet just as the vacuum tube started reacting to the disturbance.

It cracked and died. Then another. A relay tripped. Failures cascaded after one another, fuses blew, power lines shorted, and the entire low-voltage system died. The Oerfil foundry ran out of control. The electrodes retreated on residual pressure, just as the Karsik plant reconnected and the grid voltage returned to nominal.

The furnace began to roar.

When Talvek pushed the lever and disconnected the arc from the electric grid, the power draw dropped from above-design to zero within a dozen milliticks. The resulting voltage spike burst every lightbulb in the foundry. Radiating outwards from it, transformers tripped and failed. Communications equipment died altogether, their sensitive electronics burned out. Worn insulation cracked and failed, setting up fires.

Further away, between the mountains, in the defile that had given Oerfil the root to its name, an aging transformer station shook as another arc came briefly alive. It died quickly, but not before it had set the insulating oil on fire. Within minutes, the entire building was ablaze, lighting up the main walkable passage to and from Oerfil. The heated air rose in the narrow pass and began softening the snow that had accumulated on the steep slopes.

The valley went dark and deaf.

That night, the people of Oerfil woke up to a vision that most only knew from the old religious tales, if at all. The wail of sirens was underlaid by a deep, terrifying rumble, almost too low to hear. A red glow lit the clouds above the foundry, casting the rooftops and deadlight posts in ‌black relief.

The streets began to fill, first with on-duty firefighters rushing to the foundry or any of the fires springing up around the town. Constables stood ready to make sure they got there without getting mired in vehicles or walkers. Regular townspeople followed. Bakers and storekeepers ran to check their properties, but many simply wanted to see and to know.

But nobody knew yet. Confusion grew among the numbers milling on the street, and from it, rumors arose out of nothing. The Conglomerate had attacked this valley state or that, or the molten core beneath had burst through the bedrock and into the mine. A rock crashed through a store window, and a pair of fists started flying.

Mostly, however, reason prevailed. The people of ‌Oerfil may have been unused to uncertainty of this kind, but they knew uncertainty in general and had learned to plan for it from an early age. Engineers knew safety margins for low-quality materials, and storekeepers knew how to manage inventories even if shipments were late. The young man who had thrown the stone was stared down when he was about to step into the store, and the pair of would-be pugilists was pulled apart and given into the constables’ custody.

Further away, the mechanisms of the Concordat started reacting. The controllers of power had known something had happened since the explosion in Karsik, and that something was wrong since Oerfil dropped from the grid. When it didn’t come back after the standard breaker trip cycle and its operators did not reply to their hails, the response ramped up. Journeymen standing the night watch called or sent runners to drag grumbling masters from their beds. The masters called theirs until an unmanned Furnace flier was dispatched.

# Rescue

Talvek realized he was awake. It had been a gradual process, not like waking up from a dream. First came consciousness itself, a recognition of being. He didn’t know where he was or why. Only that he was awake, eyes closed. Then, feeling returned. His knee hurt, and his chest burned whenever he drew ‌a breath. A hot tingle on his face, like he had spent a day high on the slopes, skin unprotected between the spring sun and the snow. A tapping sound, like a cane, or a length of chain swaying against a wall in the breeze.

Memory came shortly after the feeling, crushing in like the weight of a mountain. Talvek forced open his eyes, eyelashes snapping free from crusty discharge.

He was in the main loading yard, just as he knew he would. It was later than he remembered, though. It was no longer dark. Not a day yet, or even a morning. Just the first light of ‌dawn, diffused by the clouds moving above.

Clouds, or smoke.

He was on the ground, half-sitting, legs splayed out. He drew his foot closer, the one behind his healthy knee, and pried himself onto his feet. He felt heavy and sluggish, like after his first days sorting scrap and before finding out he could get food from the dining hall for free, but multiplied by ten. The world threatened to spin, but a hand on a wall kept him on his feet. No, not a wall, but a pillar. The one he’d been slumped against.

There. The gatehouse, two squat towers guarding the passage through. Behind it, the market. Oerfil. The valley. _The deep rumble of a cargo train rolling in, the first he had ever seen._

He took a step. Again, the less-painful leg first. Then the other, dragged.

‘You.’

Another step. The ground swayed. Talvek adjusted his footing. A little wider. A triangle is the most stable shape.

‘You. Journeyman? Sire?’ A hand on his shoulder. Firm, like Kador’s had been. But not Kador.

Talvek turned, slowly. A blurry shape was in front of him. He blinked and squinted his eyes a few times. Gradually, the shape resolved into something recognizable. An overall, and a firefighter’s helm, a breather mask hanging on the neck.

‘Can you hear me?’ it asked. Talvek nodded, and the shape asked for his name next. He croaked his answer, and it—a middle-aged firefighter with a mustache—rewarded him with more questions. If he remembered how he ended up here and what had happened. Talvek’s voice came a little more easily now, and he told the firefighter about the blackout and the furnace. The man’s expression grew more serious.

‘You in charge?’ Talvek took a careful glance at the gate, careful not to disturb his balance, and nodded.

‘Are all workers accounted for?’ the man asked, slowly and clearly, and looked Talvek straight in the eye.

Talvek tried to remember. He had stood at the doors, shielding his eyes against the blinding light and blistering heat, vision greying as he coughed at the fumes. He had pulled the doors closed, others pulling with him, when the spill was almost at the rails. Nobody had moved inside at that point.

‘Kador went to secure the gas. Don’t know if—' he trailed off. The firefighter’s tone grew more urgent.

‘Has it been vented?’ Talvek didn’t know. Perhaps. Probably. Had to be, or it would have gone already. The spill must have reached the feed by now. The firefighter turned sideways, leaning to mutter at something on his chest. He bent his ear towards it and was quiet for a moment.

‘Can you find a floor plan somewhere? Blueprints?’ Talvek’s confusion must have shown on his face. ‘To the foundry. Someone’s tapping inside. Trapped.’

Talvek pinched the bridge of his nose. So not everybody had made it out. He should have gone around the foundry floor. Or held the door open longer. The sinking feeling was mixed with hope. Maybe it was Kador tapping.

The ground spun below his feet again, forcing him to sidestep. The firefighter grabbed his upper arm. Talvek leaned against him, grateful for the support. It was hot here. Incredibly hot. He remembered the yard being covered in frost when he last walked across it just before his bell, with little banks of snow in the corners. Now, not even moisture remained on the stones.

Behind the yard, a semi-circular wall of waist-high rocks rose, high as a cliff. It _was_ as high as _the cliff_ it had been built against. The prime tower housing the furnace. The keep. The foundry.

An old man was slowly making his way around it, periodically tapping the stones with his cane. As if one of them would reveal a secret passage , like in the stories that had been read to other kids.

A crack sounded from above. Glass. The windows were all dark grey. The small, arrow-slit sized ones closer to the ground, and likewise the large ones of paned glass high up in the newer addition of timber and sheet metal.

Filled with smoke, Talvek realized. It leaked from every vent and poorly sealed window, and from the crack now bisecting one of them. It was covering the upper floors and spreading towards the valley like a floating curtain pulled above them.

The firefighter was telling Talvek to stay calm, his tone gentle but firm, and then asked for the blueprints again.

‘They are. Inside the foundry. But I can show you. The way.’ His chest hurt deep inside with every breath, but he gestured with his arm toward the foundry for emphasis. The firefighter was lifting his hand toward Talvek’s chest, for him to stay put, but then seemed to reconsider and stopped midway through the motion. His eyes went over Talvek, methodologically, head to heels. His brow furrowed and then relaxed.

‘Appreciate it. The engineer will get you a breather. Get some water while you can. We’ll move as soon as you are ready.’

***

The heat of the loading yard was nothing compared to this. Service tunnel B seemed to stretch on forever, its walls hot to the touch. Talvek’s legs shook on the verge of caving, each tremor stabbing pain into his knee. Breathing felt like drawing hot soup into his lungs.

Under his laboring breath, he urged the firefighters to move faster and cursed when they stopped to examine the walls or the roof, seemingly for the twentieth time, measuring temperatures and tapping with small hammers. He had done simple calculations in his head; tried to remember how much heat granite could store in itself and how thick the reinforcing layer of concrete above Storage 3 was. The results were merciless. The air inside might be halfway to boilpoint already, and getting hotter by the tick.

He felt useless, no matter how much he tried to convince himself to the contrary. The service tunnels were a maze hewn into the bedrock itself behind and under the keep. Some were centuries-old shafts from the very first mine, repurposed for ventilation or maintenance. Newer additions connected them or bypassed sections where the rock had become unstable. Even Talvek was hard pressed to find the correct way. Without him, the firefighters would have spent hours without finding the storage.

It didn’t help. They didn’t even let him help carry any of the sledges or braces. Not that he wouldn’t have collapsed after a dozen steps, but still.

On his right side, a firefighter wearing a relief suit stomped on. Talvek had never seen one in person. It looked like the man was wearing a stick figure around him, like the wooden ones hanging from strings he’d seen at a traveling festival when he was a child. Only this one was made from heavy-duty steel and sheets of what he guessed was aluminum. Its footfalls shook the floor, and it carried a huge disk cutter as easily as a pry bar. Talvek found himself brushing against the wall to open the distance from it.

He was a whoreborn bastard. A worthless clinker. He should have rung the alarm before dumping the furnace, and then walked the muster points and taken names instead of standing like an idiot at the doors. Maybe then he would have had an idea of who was trapped in Storage 3. Or had been. The heat was oppressive, and a part of him dreaded what they might find when they cut through the wall. The order-of-magnitude numbers he’d run in his hazy mind didn’t help, either. Yet, a bigger part of him wanted to find out right now. He cursed at the pace again, and then at himself for his impatience. He’d done enough damage without getting them all buried under a collapsing ceiling.

At last, they reached the right girder. Talvek tapped ‌the shoulder of the man leading the rescue and then broke into a coughing fit. Bent over and convulsing, he pointed at the wall. The man nodded and turned to the wall, eyeing it and the ceiling and running his fingers against the chiseled rock split by dozens of horizontal fissures. With his sides still convulsing, Talvek watched with trepidation as the man’s expression changed from detached professionalism to concern. Again, he drew the hammer from his belt and began tapping at seemingly random spots, eyes narrowed.

Then, to Talvek’s horror, the man bent towards his radio again, repeatedly calling for somebody even as his hand made a downward motion at the rest of his party.

‘This is. The place.’ Talvek croaked. ‘Why aren’t you cutting?’

‘We need to brace the roof first. I’ve sent for a jack and some lattice girders. You’ve done well. You can follow the suit cable back to the surface.’

The floor beneath felt like Talvek might sink into it. His head sank, and he leaned against the wall for support. It was hot to the touch. They were already too late. Or they would be by the time they finally got the bracing in place. There was nothing he could do. He could beat his fists bloody against the rock, and it wouldn’t even make a dent. Not on his own.

He needed a tool. A saw. 

Talvek straightened himself. The firefighter was turning away when he gripped his sleeve.

‘No.’ Talvek’s voice was still barely more than a whisper, but the man turned back, the expression of gentle patience already forming on his face. Talvek didn’t let him speak.

‘Here. Try this wall,’ he said, dragging the man’s hand towards it.

‘It’s hot. It’s already hot.’ Talvek could see the firefighter was about to object, so he pressed on.

‘It’ll be even hotter on the other side. Storage 3’s right between us and the spill,’ Talvek continued, with what he hoped was steel in his tone. He probably didn’t succeed. In fact, he must have looked rather ridiculous. A furnace controller, barely a journeyman, arguing with a master rescuer, about a topic very much in the latter’s expertise. Still, he wasn’t about to buckle.

‘If it collapses–’

‘Then give me the saw!’ Talvek cried out, almost pleading. Then he gathered himself again and continued, his voice barely more than a whisper. ‘They’ll die otherwise.’

The firefighter was clearly about to repeat his earlier sentiment, but then seemed to reconsider. He turned towards the relief suit. It had stomped closer at some point, but Talvek hadn’t even noticed its heavy stomps.

‘Your decision. The young man’s right, I fear. But we might not get you out in time if it caves in, even if you survive the fall.’

‘I can–’ Talvek tried to cut in, but the lead firefighter silenced him with a shake of his head.

‘Saw’s almost forty kilos.’ No more explanation was offered. Nor was it needed. 
It was up to the relief suit.

Talvek turned to the hulking shape. It was impossible to see the man’s expression behind the protective visor. Even his body language was tough to read. Too much of him was hidden behind all the joints and struts. He wasn’t even sure if it was a 'he,' though he suspected so from its height, even accounting for the half a hand that the heavy boots must have added. Talvek waited, not even daring to breathe. The ticks dragged on forever.

Then, with a click and a whir, the man nodded. Talvek felt like he might collapse on the spot. Tears of relief filled his eyes. Someone grabbed his arm and began dragging him back the way they’d come.

Behind him, he heard the suit stomping a few more steps, and then a shrill whine as the saw started. It turned into a screaming roar as it bit the tunnel wall, drowning all else under itself. Dust soon filled the tunnel, stinging his eyes and coating his skin and overalls, and stealing all visibility. Talvek’s heart hammered. He had to focus, to fight against a rising wave of panic. He wasn’t in the yard, watching the door cut off the blinding glare of the spill. It wasn’t harder to breathe, either—his breather carried bottled air and had no filter for the dust to clog. When he almost succeeded at that, his mind moved on to Storage 3, conjuring up images of boiled bodies, men and women who had trusted him not to dump the furnace on them. He swallowed and squeezed his fists against the sudden wave of nauseous vertigo.

Finally, the saw stopped. For a moment, the only sound he could hear was the ringing in his ears. The dust was slowly drifting down, and he made out dark shapes moving through it. Metal scraped against rock, and then something heavy rumbled down, shaking the entire hallway. Talvek limped over the collapsed rubble and into the storage before anyone could stop him.

# Interlude

Zora decided she had sworn enough by now. Then, just to be on the safe side, she released a few more chosen expletives that she’d been saving for special occasions just like this. The boy who had brought her the prints had gone extremely pale, and was obviously trying his best to turn completely invisible. Or maybe it was a prey animal’s instinct of being utterly still. You never knew with the fresh ones. At least they had let her sleep until ‌morning. Rank had its perks, occasionally. Zora waved the boy away, and then shouted after him to bring her a coffee at his convenience, please.

The Oerfil foundry was a bright yellow smear in the high-altitude heatgraph, surrounded by a much larger patch of red-blue. The town, already a shade darker than it should have been. It would be freezing very soon unless the power came back. And it wouldn’t be coming back anytime soon, not with the burning transformer station in the slopes of the pass and the slagging chance avalanche its heat had unleashed.

But. The foundry. She kneaded ‌her temples. It was clear from the thermals that the furnace was dumped. At least, they wouldn’t have built _it_ from scratch. Whoever had the night there had made the right call. She scribbled a note on a paper to make sure they knew it, at some point when it was all over.

They weren’t completely slagged, but it was going to get excrementally difficult. The furnace was going to be out for at least two dozen weeks, probably more. The southern track would be delayed by at least as much. And as much of the nitrogen production had been routed from fertilizers to high explosives, as per unfortunate recommendations by one analyst (Prime) Zora, they’d be feeding Central with convoys of trucks for some time. It was just like the bad old times again. Life had a lovely habit of going in circles like that. 

Zora leaned back in her chair. She would have groaned, but she had to keep up the image for the journeymen sharing the centre with her. It was going to be a stiff week until they got their numbers to match again. Now, where was her damned coffee?

A few valleys towards the line, a surveyor of yields was also staring at the same high-altitude graph. She didn’t have Zora’s rank and prestige, so she _had_ been woken up in the middle of the night, the insistent ringing tearing her from between the bedsheets and her promised. He hadn’t even woken up properly, so she’d left a note on the table before tiptoeing out.

And she didn’t have Zora’s experience, or her years inuring her to situations exactly like this, so her reaction to the yellow smear was one of dawning horror. The foundry was out of operation, and its output of steel was out of the equations. Her trade was copper, true, but the logistical chains formed a finely tuned grid, and that big ‌a dent in the steel branches was bound to shake hers, too. It would take days to map out where they linked, and more to estimate how far the ripples went. Let alone actually doing something about it all.

But, it was her work. She sent a message to her lodgings–the sleepy slagger had better be up by now–explaining that she would be working late for quite some time. Well, she wouldn’t be alone in that for long. She imagined her duad-to-be would have his work cut out for him before long, too. The ripples would find their way to cartridge production, too.

# Still

Talvek needed to get away from the foundry. Just for a while. The thought had solidified slowly, growing like a slowly cooled turbine blade around a single-crystal core. And like that particular metallurgical progress, the realization had only been possible because it had been allowed to form in peace, with no outside disturbances.

And there were indeed no disturbances.

It was still. Too still. The clouds hung low, unmoving, merging seamlessly into the thin fog hugging the ground. Or maybe there was no distinction between the two, just a featureless mass of water vapor hiding the sky and every building and road alike beyond two hundred spans. Fog was common in Oerfil, but this was different and had come at the wrong time. The spill, as well as the countless small fires he’d seen the remains of, must have released who knows how many tons of silica and other particles for the vapor to condense around.

It stole all sound, too. Even his footsteps on the frosted limestone sounded subdued, like something was missing. 

It made the learned part of Talvek think of distilled water heated beyond its boiling point. Or cooled below freezing, for that matter. That also worked. As long as it was let be, it remained water. But the tiniest agitation or a grit of sand would send it assuming its natural state, either freezing solid or exploding into steam.

To the innermost part of him, the same that had warned his distant ancestors of a cave bear, or his not-so-distant forebears that the tyrant’s men were on the prowl again, it just felt insidious. Wrong and malicious. You didn’t reason with the silence. You put as much distance as you could between it and yourself before the sun got high and the silence ended, and something needed breaking.

Talvek tried to ignore that innermost part and focus on the learned one. Distilled water, a precarious equilibrium.

First, he needed to find the foundry master. To let him know he was going, to tighten that one last loose screw. He wasn’t going to just disappear again. 

If he’d only thought of that earlier, he would have been spared returning to the foundry now. After sorting out the immediate aftermath of Service B, he’d collapsed on the asphalt near the gate to the reclamation yard. Despite his exhaustion, he only caught a few winks of fitful sleep. They’d been filled with dreams, barely remembered glimpses of the lever, of the hall and the gate. After snapping awake for the fourth or fifth time, his heart hammering and the taste of soot on his tongue, he struggled to his feet and left. It would have been perfect if he could have dealt with the master there and then, but the thought hadn’t yet formed properly, so it was a moot point in any case.

Since then, he had spent what must have been hours limping around the town without a destination, gathering the tangled shreds of his thoughts. From the old center with its shingled roofs and two-story buildings of local timber and stone, to the newer parts of concrete and shiny steel and storehouses with their ceiling-high windows of green-tinted glass, it was the same. Silent and motionless, yet not calm. Not stable.

The arc being gone explained a part of it. Talvek had grown used to the low-frequency hum that the electromagnetic forces generated inside the foundry. But until now, he hadn’t realized how far into the town it had penetrated.

With his eyes cast down, and the haze, he didn’t notice the crowd until he was only a hundred spans away. It had formed on the old market square, right before the foundry itself. It made sense in retrospect; he’d seen more and more people during his wandering, and they were all going the same way. Like something was pulling them there. Most likely, it was nothing more than curiosity, or simply the fact that it would be warm there. The back of his overalls was freezing, and his breath frosted in the air before him; the heat from the foundry and the people alike would make the market the warmest spot in town. 

Talvek stopped to wipe his eyes on his sleeve. They had watered again, without any obvious cause. His face hurt, too. Badly. He angled himself to check his reflection in a nearby window. The skin was beginning to show an angry shade of red and had grown a few blisters.

Under the damage, he wore the same stupefied expression he had seen on the face of every other person his path had crossed. Eyes cast down, or seeing nothing, or flicking around as if searching for something that wasn’t there. It was like the same sense of silent unease that had the air in its grip had afflicted the people as well.

It was worse here, near the foundry. A single glance at the passers-by reflected on the glass told Talvek as much. Somebody made an unfamiliar motion with his hand, their lips moving without sound. Probably a warding gesture, a near-forgotten remnant from the times it protected against cliff devils or whatever particular brand of evil it was that used to haunt the place. Further off, a woman of Talvek’s age held a Furnace necklace clutched in her hand.

And they were all quiet. Quiet like the surrounding walls, and the very air itself. Like they were mourning. Or dead.

His throat constricted, and his eyes watered again. Briefly, he wondered if it would hurt less to blink them clear, or wipe them with the sleeve of his overalls. He simply let the pain be instead.

Two people had died on the floor when the concrete spalled. One instantly, from a flying piece of rock that had cracked his skull, and the other a short while later from similar injuries to the chest. A third apprentice had been rushed to the hospital, her upper body horribly burned.

Of Kador, there was no sign. The old foundryhand disappearing behind the conveyor was the last that anybody had seen him, and they had checked the counts thrice. He and the master of shifts, who had been one of the first senior personnel to arrive at the foundry, catching Talvek’s elbow as he stumbled out of the service tunnel.

Kador wasn’t outside the foundry, and the foundry itself must be a monoxide-filled oven by now. Nothing could live there, no human at least, unless…Unless there was a protected pocket he had forgotten or never known about, some old cistern behind spans of rock with a barred loophole open to the chill outside, for one to take shelter in, for the man who had taken him in, who had showed him the foundry and even helped him find his first place to stay, they could–

Talvek took a deep breath, exhaling slowly through his mouth. At least the two electricians trapped in Storage 3 had survived with mere heat exhaustion and mild fume poisoning. It wasn't even Talvek’s fault that they’d become trapped there, according to the master. Somebody had messed up the shift tables, and Talvek couldn’t have known they were around in the first place.

Not his fault. Talvek snorted at that, and then winced from the pain, eliciting even more pain. Good. The sheer absurdity of it remained, though. As if he had been crossing names at the musters like he was supposed to, making sure everybody who _was_ on the sheets was accounted for. And as if he hadn’t dumped a thousand tons of burning steel on them. The electricians had survived despite Talvek, and Kador had needed to go to close the gas main precisely because of him. He had doubtlessly saved dozens.

He knew his decision would be deemed correct, later. That they couldn’t have restored control in time. That the furnace would have cracked in less than an hour. That the situation had been unsalvageable the moment the lights went out.

He should have seen it all coming. But he had done what was needed, and now he would go. _Leave_. He coughed up a wad of watery phlegm and spat it on the frozen ground. It wouldn’t be like the last time. The others take it from here, once they snapped out of their current stupor. The Concordat would, too. They had processes and specialists for precisely this kind of scenario.

They had to have.

Maybe he would go tend the land up the slopes. Clean and repair the old irrigation ditches, empty the debris from the cisterns. Or tend to the beehives, fending off the rare opportunistic bear. Yes, he would like that. Perhaps he might even come back once things got back to normal.

A few people turned to watch as he reached the edge of the square and then parted to let him pass. The place was packed with people now. The square was a remnant from the old times, paved in masoned stone rather than asphalt or concrete and far too small for the town’s current size. But, together with the foundry, it formed the hearth for people to gather around, to tell stories and plan for the future. On Cooling’s Eve, it was always packed with people coming to see the fireworks. Now, the hearth was going cold.

Talvek kept his eyes on the ground before him, not willing to meet the eyes of the people. Thus, he started when somebody laid a hand on his shoulder. Just like Kador had. But it was just the storekeeper, nodding a silent greeting, or perhaps thanks. Talvek had happened upon the shattered shop window earlier, his eyes still stinging from concrete dust from the maintenance tunnel and his back drenched in sweat from the heat in the yard, and began picking up the shards of glass. He hadn’t thought much of it. He’d simply wanted to do something, anything really, but the keeper had been appreciative. He had given Talvek a carton bag full of fizzy water and some upper-shelf lunch-bread, the kind he had bought after a completed apprenticeship or a particularly hard repair. Talvek had drained the first bottle before he was at the first intersection, and the remaining two not long after. The bread rolls he’d given to someone, perhaps a family or a pair of youths, he wasn’t sure.

The sense of impending reaction was even stronger on the market, seeming to permeate the very stones of it. Nervous whispers and the shuffling of work boots and shoes on limestone filled the space. Talvek recognized an embarrassed-looking sewerworks overseer, with a black eye and a furious-looking lady who had to be his wife. Nobody seemed to give Talvek more than a glance. All eyes were transfixed on the foundry ahead, now almost entirely obscured by the smoke. 

Encouraged, he dared a few looks around him. Crust, the foundry cat, was sitting on a patch of ground that remained unfrozen even in the midst of the winter. An underground heating pipe, awaiting insulation refurbishment since before Talvek’s arrival. His eyes were half-closed and the tip of his tail flicked nervously. He had been old already when Talvek was sorting scrap, and would not take kindly to today’s disturbances to his well-polished schedule of sleeping. A little to the side, a group of a dozen people in two sets of minutely different overalls were huddled together, conversing urgently in hushed tones. Talvek recognized a loader from the foundry. The rest must be his colleagues from the rail yard, and truck drivers hauling all the tens of tons of odds and ends every day. That also explained the silence, in part. They had nothing to load now. The salaries would keep coming for a while, but not forever.

Then he was through the crowd and on the other side of the market. There was a clear taste of hot metal and burnt oil in the air, and it was warmer, just as he’d known it would be. The frost had disappeared from the stones under his feet. His breath still misted in clouds whenever he exhaled, heavier than they had been in the town. Humidity from all the melted snow, and the cloud cover that seemed to hug the ground up here, most likely. A damp chill. He could almost smell moldy cloth and rotting floorboards again.

The foundry looked…damaged. Broken, even. Smoke-fog obscured the entire upper wing, as well as the newer floors added vertically over the old keep. The lower wing protruding from the curtain wall looked desolate, and the minor yard around was barred. He had never seen them empty of people. The damned quiet was really getting into him, too.

Talvek spotted the foundry master some distance to his left. He was accompanied by several Furnace men and women Talvek knew were handling the logistics of the continuous streams of ore and steel and everything else that was needed to keep the furnace running. Had been needed. Each pair of eyes in the group was fixed on the foundry. None spoke. None moved, either. Only periodic puffs of vapor marked them as still alive.

Talvek felt his heart rate pick up. This was it. The final rivet. His feet continued to carry him forward, and he swallowed to clear his throat. It felt raw, and dry. Weird, he hadn’t realized it before.

He had just reached the group and was opening his mouth to speak when another voice beat him to it.

‘Never thought I would see that again.’ Decades of use had worn the voice rough. It belonged to an old man, leaning against a cane some distance from the master and the rest of the group. Talvek gasped, thinking for a heartbeat that it was Kador. But it wasn’t, of course. The instant of joy deflated like an abandoned bellows, and Talvek with it. The man was old, too old to be Kador.

He was staring at the foundry, and continued muttering to himself. He murmured, so quietly that his breath didn’t even mist when he did, yet his voice carried.

‘Prayed the gods I never would, I did.’ He was wearing an old overall, worn rough and patched many times over. It hung loose over his frame.

The forge master paid no attention to the man. His eyes remained on the smoke-covered building.

‘Well?’ The old man’s voice was suddenly demanding. He tapped his cane on the paving. In the silence, it sounded like a hammer hitting a rivet. It made Talvek start. The light caught on something in the man’s hand as he adjusted his grip. An iron ring, Talvek saw. Nicked and corroded, but unmistakably iron.

‘Are we just going to wait until the entire Foundry freezes to death?’

The elder was clearly confused, probably riven awake in the midst of his sleep. Freezing was not going to be a problem any time soon. If anything, the master would certainly like the killing heat from the spill to bleed out faster, for the recovery work to begin.

Then the realization hit. It was so obvious that he might have felt his cheeks heating, had they not been burning already. The man was speaking about the town, or the administrative region comprising the entire valley. Of course. The Foundry with a capital initial. The town, and the valley, and the people, in speech and on paper both. Talvek had always found that term as a source of confusion, but the born natives took it in stride. Talvek should have, too; he’d definitely had enough practice.

Then the actual realization hit, and all levity disappeared. Talvek’s stomach turned leaden, and the air suddenly felt freezing indeed. The beehives would have to wait.

‘He’s right,’ he muttered. It came out as barely a whisper. The master remained unmoving, staring at the foundry. He had to see. Talvek had to make him see, like he himself did, cracks branching and failures cascading. What little time they had was leaking into the cold air and the frozen rock by the tick. If the sewers froze and burst, they might even have plague on their hands.

‘He’s right,’ he urged, louder this time and even grabbing the front of the master’s jacket. His highest superior blinked and then turned to regard him, eyebrows rising in recognition. 

‘The heat’s gone.’ Talvek pointed at the smoke-wreathed foundry, right past the old man, his tone almost pleading. As if on cue, ice crunched under his boot, like broken glass on polished bedrock. The master’s brow furrowed, as if still not comprehending. Then his eyes focused again as the understanding hit.

‘The heat,’ he repeated, brow furrowed, mouth working even after the audible sentence. Then he began listing tasks and queries to his staff. The frozen indecision was gone, and he was back to being the man Talvek had seen when a freight train was delayed or an electrode unexpectedly cracked, or any of the dozen minor crises Talvek had already seen him handle. Next, one of the logistics women dug out a slate and began tapping the keys, head dipped and eyes narrowed in concentration. In his mind, Talvek pictured the first crystal of ice forming, the master, and the typing woman in its vertices. A man from the staff followed suit, murmuring urgently into a radio.

The nearest hangers-on turned to watch too, suddenly attentive. The equilibrium had been disturbed.

# Interlude

Outside the valley, the ripples spread. Controllers of the rail network worked frantically as the lights on the board turned yellow and then red. Oerfil had been receiving over one hundred and twenty train cars of processed ore and scrap steel every day, and sending back nearly as many laden with heavy slabs of steel. Suddenly, those cars weren’t moving into Oerfil anymore. Phones rang constantly to keep the tracks free and the trains moving. Together with the Furnace planners and surveyors, trainloads of ore were hastily rerouted, and the steel that still was being produced was reallocated between the various foundries and mills.

Some consequences had the potential to turn sinister, even if they started from entirely calculated actions. A smeltery, barely into commission, was shut down as part of the first attempts at stabilizing the chains of supply and demand. Confused workers, new to their trade, gathered in the central square. A young preacher was delivering a fiery sermon, driven by righteous vindication. The Mountain was taking its revenge and bringing down the defilers’ blasphemous temple, but it was not too late to repent. A half-empty plastic bottle flew, catching his shoulder.

‘My kids got food in them bellies now!’ Several others shouted their assent or punched the air in support. The preacher, uncowed, doubled down on his sermon, oblivious to the workers edging closer. Hands closed into fists, or began searching for something heavier than a bottle.

The old preacher arrived just then to shake hands and nod sagely. His hip had started acting up abruptly, so he would appreciate a few helping hands with the soup kettle. Besides, Qvell was still missing, and somebody needed to check the hospital and his typical haunts. Oh, and the throng of the cargo train cars that had filled the yard, just in case. One by one, the crowd followed him out of the square.

# Cells

It was like being in the screaming control chamber once more. He couldn’t pull the lever again.

Not again.

He was trapped. And this time there would be no strange men arriving in trucks and making the lights come back and the trains start rolling.

The rage hit him like an avalanche. He smashed his forehead against the timber wall, as hard as he could, and screamed through his ground-together teeth. It was so shitting-fever unfair. Had he not caused enough suffering already?

Had he not suffered enough himself?

The scream ended as the air in his chest ran out. He slid down against the wall, turning like an inclined shaft-joint as he did, so that he ended up sitting on the plank porch with his back against the logs. Maybe the old local tales had been right. Maybe the cliff fiends did exist, and had singled him out as a target for their evil-doing. Or maybe the furnace itself had cursed him through some yet-undiscovered mechanism of space and time, dooming him to repeat his non-decision until his days ran out.

Talvek let his eyes unfocus as he stared into the distance. There was nothing to see. It was too dark, and the clouds would have covered them anyway, but he knew the peaks were there. The peaks, and the trails in between. A way out. He had been so tempted to just go.

But he couldn’t make himself do it. First, he needed to solve this problem. Always something to break down, indeed. He banged the back of his head against the logs, for good measure. But it was lackluster, just going through the motions. He barely felt the impact.

Then he ran out of feeling altogether, like the ore mines upvalley had run out a hundred years ago, or so he’d heard. Talvek sat there, cold timber under and behind him, staring at nothing in particular. The sunrise was still hours away, though the moon provided some illumination beyond the clouds. Not much, it was too dark to even read the arm-high text on the signboard barely a dozen spans away. The upper end of the staircase, itself nothing more than centuries-old natural stone worked into the ground of the slope, disappeared into the darkness. Closer, he could just make out how lichen had begun to grow over the smooth-polished stone.

Gradually, exhaustion crept back in. He began glimpsing things at the edge of his vision, or further away where the dark overpowered everything else. Heavy bed cloth hanging from a makeshift broomstick frame. A mattress leaned against the staircase, patterned with an intricate floral that had faded with time into near-invisibility.

Talvek squeezed his eyes shut and then forced them wide open. The mattress disappeared. The hallucinations had first come on the second night. Or was it the third? He wasn’t sure. The nights were many, and they all blurred together. The visions came in the dark, whenever he sat down or stood still for too long. He knew they were waking dreams, brought about by too little sleep. Mundane objects and structures where none existed, if he was lucky. Talvek didn’t mind those. If he wasn’t lucky, he saw a shape in overalls walking into a rain of liquid fire, slow but uncompromising. Those visions always sent him flinching back, slapping his cheeks or cupping up fistfuls of loose snow from a bank and throwing them across his face.

It never drove them away for long. Knowing that it was a natural reaction, his sleep-deprived brain making up things, didn’t either. Busying himself with the work did help for a while, as did using a bright light or a well-illuminated spot to force himself into a higher state of wakefulness. In time, sleep would cure them entirely.

He wanted to sleep so badly. His head was leaden, and his eyelids kept creeping shut no matter what he did. If he stopped fighting it, he’d probably shut down right where he sat and freeze halfway to death. If he was lucky. But sleep would have to wait.

Until then, he would likely see blankets and mattresses any time he rested his eyes on a batch of featureless concrete, or on a shadowed slope of earth, like he had just moments ago.

He swung his head back against the wall again and closed his eyes for a tick, the risk of freezing be damned. He had thought it was done. The old lady’s house just behind the spur should have been the very last one, though he would have spent the next day double-checking the sheets and knocking on doors at random, just to be sure. But it should have been done. The immediate threat of exposure had been dealt with. 

Talvek had dealt with it. 

Starting from the moment on the market, with the old man and the foundry master, the entire town had solidified into action, Talvek in the midst of it. It was all movement and no plan in the beginning, like a runaway forklift going in circles.

Normally, waste heat from the foundry accounted for more than two-thirds of the thermal energy needed. Even if the power had stayed on, its absence would have been sorely felt. With both the foundry and the power cold, the situation was dire indeed.

They didn’t see the danger. It had taken Talvek some time to realize it, but once he did, it was painfully obvious. Oh, they understood it with their learned part, just like they understood anything that was thrown in front of them. Half of a sentence, and they’d take a tick to consider it, and then nod in understanding, their expressions severe.

But they didn’t feel it in their innermost core. Not like Talvek did. To them, cold was something you put on your thickest coat for, while you walked somewhere warmer or had a steaming mug of spiced wine on the square. Not something that crept in when the firewood ran out or the chimney needed sweeping. Slowly at first, but unstopping and unstoppable, seeping down into your bones and bringing with it the damp, and the fever if you were unlucky.

Talvek couldn’t make them see, but he made them do as though they did. He’d raised his voice, pointed with his finger, even wrenched a few sleeves. Again, a moment’s consideration followed by an understanding. They’d attached the task with greater vigor since.

Oh, there was a lot other damage to repair, and a rockslide or an avalanche had blocked the defile itself. That took most of the person-power available, but Talvek had been purely focused on the heat problem. Together with others, they had quickly designed an intricate heat exchanger system to tap into the heat of the slowly solidifying lake of metal inside the foundry and then route it to the central grid. 

That was until the old science teacher ‌found out and slapped some sense into them, together with a lesson on the magnitude of heat available versus that needed to keep a town of this size warm. Talvek should have seen the mistake. It was what he was good at, and doubly so when it included the natural laws of conduction and capacity that made up such a large portion of his trade. Without the teacher, they could have wasted days they didn’t have.

They couldn’t do that, couldn’t keep the homes warm, so they settled for keeping the people from freezing instead. Talvek didn’t know who had come up with the idea. Rumor had it that somebody had seen the kids play Break the Wall, and that had sparked the idea in their heads. Or, that a Hammer survival specialist had arrived from the outside, becoming the first person in recorded history to cross the Barteth Mountains with an aircraft. Or anything in between, depending on who you asked.

Talvek suspected the truth fell somewhere between those extremes. If he really had to pick, he was inclined to agree with the former theory, as far as his fuzzy mind could agree to anything. He had spent the last however-many days building what were very much like the pillow-and-mattress fortresses he knew kids were supposed to make whenever a parent could be made amenable. Heat cells, they were called. Built from blankets, curtains, and whatever material was available, they were a tiny home within a home, with a vastly reduced surface area for heat to leak through. Even body heat would keep them tolerable indefinitely, and most houses had wood-burning steamrooms, the fuel of which could be stretched to last for months if needed.

Somebody had printed instructions for the heat cells on leaflets, and Talvek had helped, among others, to get them distributed. Most could build their own cell, and expected to do so, but there were always exceptions. Old people, like the lady whose house should have been the last one. People with infirmities, small children with their parents in critical roles, only coming home to snatch a few hours of sleep, if even that. Well, the children had needed no help whatsoever in Talvek’s opinion. Building a mattress fortress was an innate skill for them, and Talvek’s role had been reduced to lifting the heaviest mattresses or reminding them that the fort needed to be armored against the cold seeping in from the floor and not just the sides and top. A few older kids had even joined him as he moved to the next house or apartment on his list, to join another bunch of kids in building their fort, or doing the building altogether for some old man or lady.

Talvek had done well with the heat cells. Excelled, even, he liked to think, when his fluctuating brain chemistry decided he could feel a little more than nothing for a minute. He had refined the craft of building them, even devising a simple little spacer that could be cut out of plywood or fiberboard in a minute. Propped between two layers of curtains or bedsheets, it created a little airspace, wide enough to leverage the insulating properties of air but not so wide that they were negated by the temperature difference circulating the gas. He’d hobbled around the town to share his idea with the other cell-groups, and even got the latest set of printed instructions amended with a schematic.

He’d been too fast. That was the root cause. He’d been so close, but he had been too fast.

He had gotten his allotment of cells built too fast, and thus arrived at the planners’ tent–and thus became the only Cell Team Overseer (temporary) there at the moment–just as the surveyor brought the sheets from the rest home.

And Talvek had seen the problem right away. Seen the impossible choice laid bare, and how little time they had left to come up with a solution. Of course he had.

He’d been so close to getting away from Oerfil. Oh yes, he needed to get out of the valley entirely. He must have been too dumbfounded earlier, thinking that just a little distance to the foundry would do. His lip curled at the thought. He had made that mistake too many times, in the years before, crawling back time after time, but no more. There were other valleys beyond, with other slopes and other trades for him to learn. No need to even wait for the trains to return. There were tortuous mountain trails that he knew joined Oerfil to the adjacent rivers, at least for anyone fit and brave enough to risk them. He had double-checked his backpack on his way there. Some clothes and a blanket, matches. A little food, though he didn’t want to take away any more than necessary. Besides, he remembered how to be hungry just alright. Like riding a bike, as they said in Oerfil, though he had never got the chance to learn, in his life before. Dangerous, especially with his damaged knee, but then again, jumping onto a moving train hadn’t been exactly safe, either.

‘Still building cells, eh?’ The voice raised Talvek from his thoughts. He had no memory of getting up, yet he had obviously done so, and walked a while besides. He was standing on the Steelroad, that much was immediately clear.

He had learned of it during his early years in Oerfil. Countless sleepless nights, desperately catching up years of missed learning, on sums and letters, and then on heat and metals and power. Yet, he had found a little time to study Oerfil itself, to…he wasn’t sure why, really.

But thanks to it, he knew that the steel from the foundry had been carted down the long, serpentine path to the river, to be traded off with other valleys and passing barges. As the foundry grew, a cable rail had been constructed to handle the bulk of the material transport. Processed ore went up, and steel came down. Yet, the road had remained busy. Trucks carried locally sourced scrap and the occasional spare component uphill, and brought down steel for the local forges and smiths. Workers met at the numerous cafeterias, eateries, and taverns that had sprung up around the winding turns, and mingled with the rest of the valley folk, or the occasional traveler, or a caravaneer staying at an inn.

Talvek took a tick to gather his surroundings. He was almost exactly midway between the foundry and the rail yard, though he had no idea how he had gotten there. He had been sitting there, against the wall, and it had been pitch black then. Now, the clouded sky above him was already turning from black-grey on one side of the valley to white on the other. Talvek guessed he should have felt dismayed about losing time like that, but the feeling just wouldn’t come. Another thing for later, when he had the time for it. 

Like his knee, still swollen and only bending a little. He’d let a doctor check it when she’d spotted him limping in the confusion of the first days. She'd been adamant on him resting, even following him a little and never once shutting up, until Talvek had snapped at her. He had work to do. At least the pain had declined into a dull ache, from its unbearable peak a few nights ago, when the knee was an inflamed mass of pain that refused to bend at all.

‘Hmm?’ A hand on Talvek’s shoulder, and the same voice again, wordless and patchy like static over a radio, bringing Talvek back to the present. Again. ‘The cells?’

It was the old man from the market, standing at Talvek’s elbow. Their paths had crossed two or three times since then, but Talvek had already learned a lot about the man. Malk, he was called. He’d been a scrap sorter for forty years in another foundry, and then retired here into a cottage or a chalet left by a distant relative. Although Malk had never said as much, Talvek had gathered he’d seen a furnace go cold at least once before. It was evident from the familiarity, or rather the resigned ease, with which he handled every new obstacle.

‘Did the last one this morning.’ Malk leaned in, expecting Talvek to continue, and then nodded when no further information was coming. The pair settled into a comfortable silence. It was not the earlier silence of the square, the kind that presaged activity, or worse, but rather a simple lack of speech in the bustle of activity going on around them. The shadows grew shorter–the shadowless grey all around them grew uniformly less-shadowy–and the sky turned a brighter shade of featureless gray. Only a week ago, the road would have been getting rapidly busier. The first trucks would have stopped for fuel or a coffee, or just a word and the latest meaningless rumour.

Only a week, and the entire town had seemingly grown beyond the concept of time. Day or night, a substructure of frantic activity underlaid everything. As he watched, a trio of people crossed the street a few dozen spans to his left, greeting Talvek by his name. Their breath puffed in small clouds of vapor. Two plumbers and a carpenter, by their looks, steaming mugs of coffee or tea in their hands, on their way to tackle the day’s share of new, minor crises. To his right, from where a soft scent of wood smoke was drifting, a group stood by an eatery, built where the road bent. They were huddled together against the cold, bowls of stew or broth in their hands, most likely. Their crises were over for the day, or for their shift, as the concept of a day didn’t really apply anymore, only to be replaced by new ones after a few insufficient hours of rest.

It went beyond the immediate needs of rescue and recovery. He thought back to _that house_ with a shudder, the rambling rages about atonement while coming off a bender, this god or that requiring deeds of service for any perceived transgression. Maybe it hadn’t been that delirious after all. It certainly seemed to apply to the entirety of Oerfil now. To a degree, at least. The centuries-old shrines carved into the bedrock lining the road opposite Talvek remained as cold and untended-to as ever.

‘How’s the water?’ Talvek gestured at the slate Malk carried under his left arm. The old man had volunteered himself for logging the readings from the various water meters across Oerfil. The water system was fed from an aquifer upslope, and apparently would keep running even with the power out, but at a reduced capacity. Talvek didn’t know if it was entirely gravity-driven, or if some of the windmills there were powering the pumps. In any case, water had gone from a commodity into a resource to be carefully monitored. Too much usage, and they’d start draining the reservoirs. Too little flow, on the other hand, and the pipes would freeze and burst. People like Malk, best spared from the hardest physical toil yet still fit enough to walk, were logging and carrying readings to the controllers of water, and knocking on doors where consumption fell outside tolerances.

‘Still flowing. When’s the last time you slept, though? You look like slag.’ Talvek added the unspoken _boy_ to the end of the sentence, and almost winced from the flush of guilt and grief. So there were some things that he still could feel, obviously. He gathered himself before the silence grew and made up an answer to Malk, adding a nap or two here or there. Malk looked at him knowingly and then changed the topic.

‘Heard they’ve almost cleared the valley now, and will get trackbeds through in a day. Fuel, and transformer parts for the station,’ Malk said and waved his cane in the general direction of the defile. It was hazel, it seemed, warm yellow-brown with darker spots. Then he chuckled and shook his head, smiling. ‘Either that, or we’ll be cut off until ‌spring. Depends on whom you ask.’ 

Talvek made a small chuckle at that, but then simply watched as the sun began to light the slopes beyond the road. The fog was thinner than usual, and he could see all the way to the old ore mines. No more than vague repressions under the snow-covered brush now. A flock of mountain sheep was grazing there, their stocky build and thick wool well suited to ploughing furrows into the snow to get to the frozen grass below. Every few ticks, a ram lifted its heavy-horned head, keeping a diligent watch for wolves or a competing male. A step, a mouthful of frozen moss, and again, and then again. Did it ever want to get away?

‘Something’s bothering you.’ It was a statement. Yet, also a question, and comforting. Malk was watching Talvek intently now. One of his eyes was milky, Talvek noticed only now, before turning his eyes away. Malk’s stare was digging into him, it felt.

‘The rest home.’ Malk’s silence was as much a question as ‌his statement had been. ‘We…we can’t build cells there. Not feasible. They can’t move very well. Need too much care.’

‘Can’t they be moved to the hospital? Surely–’

Talvek explained it all. Once started, the words spilled out easily, like oil from a discharge valve. The rest home had turned out to be the inverse of the heat cell work. With a few exceptions, the inhabitants couldn’t move in and out of a cell small enough to retain warmth, and generated relatively little body heat to begin with. It was the same in the hospital, he had learned, but the hospital had backup generators and fuel to last for weeks. The rest home didn’t. Even now, cold would be slowly seeping in through the floorboards, slowly but inexorably. Talvek crossed his arms around himself at the thought.

It was stupid, though. Had there been no solution, they would have simply moved the people to the hospital. Probably. Talvek hadn’t studied that option further. It would have been crowded, that much was sure.

The problem wasn’t that they couldn’t heat the rest home. The problem was that they could, and that they could not.

‘Go on.’

‘We could wait. It’s a big building, and the stones retain enough heat for a week. If they get the pass open, and generators through…’

‘Wouldn’t want to take that gamble, myself. Sounds like a proper slag-tangle in the pass.‘

Talvek had spoken to one of the overseers clearing the pass. They were quite confident they would get the defile cleared enough for high-priority items to roll through within four days. That would still allow for the inevitable requisition delay and a problem or two in interfacing them with the home, but with all of it together, it was eating their margins for error uncomfortably thin.

Which brought Talvek to the second option.

‘We can use the Glassmaker’s. Break it for parts.’

Its full name was the Glassmaker’s Shrine. Another lesson from his studies, and even Kador had brought it up once or twice. A few really saw it as something like a shrine. Not exactly like the centuries-old niches lining the Steelroad, carved into the rock itself, but something along that general outlook. Talvek wasn’t sure how their particular brand of worldview worked, exactly.

Most people knew it simply as the Glassmaker’s, though. Originally a glassworks, it had turned into a museum of sorts, and a permanent fixture of Oerfil. 

And Talvek was going to smash its rear wall down and tear apart the kilns and pipes and fans inside. The rest home was close enough that they could haul even the biggest parts there and patch the kiln into the care home’s own heat circulation system. Ridiculous as it was, Oerfil was critically low in parts, and the Maker’s was the best they had. The only they had, in terms of unused machinery just lying around. He had gone through the inventories twice.

‘Not sure I see the problem.' Malk scratched his head, eyebrows raised.

Talvek wasn’t sure, either. He’d tried telling himself as much, that it was just an emergency measure. He didn’t have a choice, and they could restore the shrine after everything was back to normal. But try as he did, the very thought made him break a cold sweat and feel like he was breathing furnace fumes.

‘It’s just…ah. I don’t know. Like I’ve destroyed enough already.’

A pause, and Talvek continued.

‘I was planning to leave, once the cells were built. But now I have to do this first.’

Again, they both stood silent.

‘Sounds like y’need this.’ Malk dug something from his pocket, cane held under his arm. A metal flask, full of scratches and dents. The cap made a little clink against Malk’s ring as he opened it with his other hand. He proffered the bottle to Talvek.

Talvek took a sip, bracing for the pungent taste of root juice or potato still or whatever name this particular brand of near-toxic local bootleg went by.

It wasn’t that. It was strong, true, the evaporating alcohol burning his nasal cavities and making his eyes water. But it was good-quality stuff, he could tell. A hint of smoke and cedar resin hit his taste first, followed by a finish of herbs he didn’t recognize.

The surprise must have shown on his face, for Malk chuckled.

‘You like?’ the old man asked, his ravine-crossed face breaking into a smile.

‘It’s…yes. It’s good. Very good.’ Talvek trailed off.

‘Not what you expected?’ Before Talvek could answer, he laughed and then continued. ‘Not my usual pick, either.’

Malk turned to gaze into the distance. 

‘Someone gave it to me once.’ His voice was softer now. Delicate, even. ‘Been saving it for a long time.’ Talvek barely heard his next words. ‘This is all that’s left.’ Malk swished the bottle for emphasis.

They both stood quietly for a while. Talvek watched as a single snowflake fell, spinning through the unmoving air and joining the cloud of his misted breath. Malk offered the flask again, but Talvek declined.

‘You should save it for yourself.’

Now Malk laughed.

‘Been saving it long enough,’ he said and took a gulp himself.

Again, they both stood still. Talvek felt the first buzz of alcohol, despite only having taken a small sip. Tiredness, he reasoned.

Malk’s words had made him think, though. The bottle of alcohol—he must have decanted it at some point—obviously represented something important to the old man. A special someone, or friends, or a place—it didn’t matter. It meant something to Malk, yet he chose not to make it last forever, but to enjoy it while it did.

Talvek just couldn’t shake the feeling that it was important, somehow. That if he could make one more connection, it would all make sense. But try as he did, clarity refused him.

He let the thought be, a mental equivalent of a shrug, and turned to thank Malk for the liquor.

But the old man had already gone, like he’d never been there at all. Only the faint taste of herbs remained.

Talvek stood for a moment longer, thinking of nothing in particular, and then began retracing the single set of footsteps on the frost-covered stone, uphill back towards the rest home and the foundry.

***

Talvek stood in the rubble of broken bricks and sawdust. An irregular hole in the wall marked where they dragged the kiln and the draft fan machinery. Cracked foundations and empty boltholes surrounding a lighter area in the floor were the only evidence they had ever been there.

His breath still rattled in his chest from the exertion, though he had barely lifted a mallet himself. But he was warm from all the moving around, and his knee had stopped hurting. Blood flow flushing out the pain poisons and carrying fresh oxygen into the tissue. Even the sun had found a small gap in the clouds, making the ice crystals in the frost sparkle for a moment.

Yet all that Talvek really saw around him was the ruin. The air smelled of powdered brick, and mortar crunched under his soles any time he shifted his weight, crumbling into powder. A shard of glass cracked.

But it was done. It had left him feeling tarnished, but it was done. Right now, his team was rigging the kiln into the rest home heating system. Once they got the pipes mated and a shed built to protect it against the elements, it could feed on whatever combustible material could be scavenged. They had beaten the cold.

He could go now. His table was clean, all parts fitted, polished, and in-place. Just one more check with the team, nodding his approval for work well done, and then the backpack and the slopes.

There was a part that didn’t sit well with him, true. He kept thinking back to the last time. Rubble and ruin had surrounded him then, too, as he sneaked out in the dark, careful not to crack the broken glass on the floor, or bump against the pieces of cheap, destroyed furniture littered around.

But it was different now. The people here could look after themselves. Soon the snows would melt, and they had an entire society supporting them. They wouldn’t miss one man, barely more than a boy. 

Farming. That’s what he had decided. Maybe he could shepherd the crag sheep. He liked animals, though he would need to get his knee sorted out well enough for him to dodge when the rams got all head-butty in the autumn. Heh. Or maybe he could raise…what were they called now? Parsnips. That was the word here. He had never had them before, and it had taken a while before he had developed a liking for the earthy taste.

The present snapped back.

It was suddenly too quiet again, prompting Talvek to swallow. The sounds of work from the rest house had stopped. They might have finished already, but there should have been something. The scrape of a tape measure against stone as an overseer made the final checks, and the indistinct murmur of numbers called and verified.

His sweat was cold against his back now, but it was not because of the quiet alone. Before the quiet, he had heard a crack.

A loud crack, sharp but also deep and powerful. Something big and strong giving to something even stronger.

The crack of rock that had never cracked before. And it had come from the foundry.


# Interlude

Within a week, the ripples from that single cold furnace in Oerfil had spread to the furthest reaches of the Concordat. Many places were already seeing second- or third-order effects, disturbances in supply disturbing demand, triggering a cascade there or a stockpile collapse here. Every level of ‌society would witness at least a minor influence on its daily lives, even if it didn’t actively register it or be aware of its root cause.

For the common folk, empty or half-stacked shelves were the most visible effects, along with delayed deliveries of goods and services. Invisible to most, massive efforts were being taken to keep ‌everyday life running as low-friction as it did. While the surveyor of yields worked sleepless nights to keep her part of the copper supply network from collapsing, a merchant simply gave up on buying a copper pail for his steamhouse. Copper had always been the material of his childhood, but after hearing that his expected delivery had been delayed indefinitely, he finally caved in and went for aluminum instead.

Occasionally, a little good came out, too. In Northern, Paros stood by the main loading doors of his cooling foundry, cane on gravel, nodding his goodbyes. He had sent his workers home early for the second time within a year, and for the second time during all his decades as a foundry master. He didn’t have much say in the matter, and he saw the logic all too well himself. The influx of steel had been severely limited, and would remain so for some time indeed. Rather than run all the forges at partial capacity, it was much more optimal, or rather, less suboptimal, to let the most suitable ones run unimpeded, curtail the others, and shut some down altogether. As it was still undergoing the latest stages of retrofitting, Paros’s forge was a logical candidate for the latest category.

He turned on the spot, intent on doing one final round around the calibers and honing stations before switching off the lights and locking the main gate. His cane had just left the ground when he noticed it. Shapes were moving by the northern wall. His sight wasn’t what it used to be, and it was already getting darker, but he recognized his people. Workers and overseers, both. Paros hurried forward to see, cane barely touching the ground. A weird hope was building inside him. He had made the remark offhand, more to himself than to anybody else. He hadn’t expected anybody to take it up. The times were different, and the shifts were longer. Nobody would stay and spend unpaid hours to…paint. The cans and brushes being carried to the wall told him otherwise. An overseer was standing on a ladder and sketching the outline of the mountains on the wall. When Paros reached the wall, he stopped his work and greeted the master. Paros only nodded back, not trusting his voice just yet. His eyes were already searching for a piece of chalk.

Naturally, shutting down a specialized forge like Paros’s sent its own ripples downstream. The breacher armor was built around a hydraulic actuation system, high on maintenance and requiring a steady supply of spares. But the capacity and production levies had been partially lifted, as yet another temporary measure to stabilize the situation. Thus, the chamber of makers running the forges in Paros’s tributary of Northern had optimized for their immediate horizon.

It was way beyond that horizon that the weight of the decision was felt in full. In the southeastern lines of alignment, not that far from the Karsik-Koharsk line, Taren dipped his head a fraction, in approval, as the company master brought the news. Component stockpiles had run critical, and an alignment was put on hold. In the immediate military-tactical theater, it was a perfectly logical decision, and what Taren would have decided himself, had he ever aspired to that level of responsibility. It was a single-valley state, and only tortuous mountain passes connected it to the fiefdoms curving around it. As such, it could hardly be used as a fallback position or as a staging area for a flanking attack. However, though this was unknown to the local command at the time, the mountains around had a small but significant deposit of tin. This deposit had already been counted in the projections for brass, an alloy already marked as concerning by the respective Furnace planners and surveyors. An even larger share of existing production would be allocated for cartridge production, a priority demand to keep the various alignment operations running, and programs would be initiated to monitor sidestreams and to control wastage.

There would be no spare pieces for a first apprentice to machine a gear-jewel out of.

# Furnace

They had found Kador’s body three days ago. The spill had cooled enough to briefly enter the foundry again, starting from the preprocessing wings and loading hangars, comparatively distant from the furnace. A party of volunteers, clothed in heat-resistant overalls and with tanks for their closed-circuit breathers on their backs, had ventured all the way to the access hatches to the furnace hall. Ostensibly, it had been to gather readings on temperature and gas composition, but everybody knew that was a grease-polish only. Many had liked the foundry-hand, and he was the only missing row in the shift sheets at that point. Finding him was on everyone’s mind.

And found him they had. Propped against the wall on top of an overturned tool locker, his hand still wrapped around the lever. The liquid metal had reached the floor below him, scorching his overalls and blackening the skin on his outstretched hand. But, by shutting the fist-thick steel hatch, Kador had barred it from reaching the next room and the gas main therein. If the melt had breached the pipe, the resulting explosion would have cascaded all the way to the main tanks, ‌collapsing the keep itself onto the yard and the evacuating workers.

Kador’s actions had doubtlessly saved dozens, and the foundry itself, but they shouldn’t have cost his life. The hatch should have closed automatically the moment Talvek pulled the lever, or at the very least Kador should have been able to take shelter in the gas room after pulling the emergency override. The word around was that the spool valve had been leaking pressure, requiring someone to stay behind and keep the lever pulled. And that someone had been Kador. Talvek would have wanted to be the one to find him, true, but a part of him was relieved he had been spared the sight. At least it had likely been the fumes that had killed him in the end, and not the heat. Likely.

With an effort, Talvek pushed the thoughts from his mind. He needed to focus now. He was in the main yard again, walking towards the foundry. The old keep seemed higher than before, its old stones rising high as the mountains themselves, or so it felt. An ugly crack bisected three of the rocks, some distance from the gate towards the right wing. A sub-mil gauge wouldn’t have fit in it, but its mere presence still felt like an act of violation. Like something had permanently broken and could never be repaired.

But the true damage was below, hidden from sight. The spill had been too much for the bedrock. Subjected to heat utterly unnatural in its intensity and locality, the rock had heaved and cracked like shale from the uneven expansion. The seismic maps–Talvek had placed the charges closest to the foundry himself–had been grim reading. They’d need to support the rock itself with steel pins and replace the heat-crumbled lime mortar from the keep foundations with reinforced concrete.

Repairing the foundations alone would take months, and they could only begin after they had cleared the spill. And they had to complete everything before the snows fell and the ground froze again, for the concrete to cure and for Oerfil to not eke out an entire winter without heat. Which was why Talvek was going in now.

He would have preferred to go in yesterday, when everything was in place and there were still some hours to spare before ‌sunset. But the master had taken one look at Talvek and told him to get some sleep first before returning to his papers. For once, Talvek hadn’t even felt like arguing. The sun was rising when he woke up to a girl shaking his shoulder. By her expression, she’d been at it for some time already. Every cubic mil of his body hurt, but after shaking off the worst of the grogginess, his mind was clearer than ever. He’d rolled up the heavy blanket that had kept him warm, and fixed it into his backpack.

By the engineers’ calculations, and the preliminary measurements they were based off, the air in the furnace hall was still hotter than a steamroom, and heat emitted by the spill was even worse. Talvek had no reason to doubt the estimates. He’d begun shivering in moments when he stripped to his undergarments, in a stiff morning breeze and the air still well below freezing after the night’s cold, and into an equally cold protective overall. His teeth had clattered without a stop until he reached the yard. Now, though, he was getting comfortable. The flagstones paving the ground were free of frost, and the main gate, though still spans away, felt like the first sun of spring on what little skin of his face remained uncovered.

He was going to miss the cold very soon, he knew.

In addition to the heat, a thousand dangers might still be lurking around. A thin crust might hide a deep pool of still-liquid steel, a load-bearing girder might have been warped by the heat, a bubble of oxygen-starved air might have been trapped by thermal blanketing… The list was endless. But the work needed to start now, and for that, more and better measurements were needed, and experienced eyes needed to see the hall.

Eyes like Talvek’s. Though only just out of apprenticeship when compared to the foundry master or the highest overseers, he still had years of experience working in the furnace hall. Besides, he had been there the moment it all happened, and might thus more easily spot something out of place. _And having the foundry master plunge into a hidden pool of liquid fire would be a waste of his decades-long experience._ But Talvek had needed no convincing. It was his choice that had brought all this down, and no amount of convincing by the master or the control system engineers–or Talvek himself–that it had been the right one would bring back Kador, or the other dead, or make up for the however many months of production they had lost. No, he needed to be here.

Talvek stopped at the gate. It arced above him and the sliding doors, open by barely a forearm’s length. The spill had warped the tracks, and the doors wouldn’t move. They had been wide open otherwise, to speed up the cooling. The slit of the foundry inside, visible through the slit, was coke-black. The sky behind him was still covered in a uniform layer of clouds, and Talvek’s eyes had gotten used to the diffuse, shadowless light it threw. They would soon get used to the low light inside, he told himself, and he had a light on his belt.

Still, he hesitated. He had to check his breather for leaks, and the meters in his satchel needed…Ah slag it, he could at least be honest with himself. He didn’t want to return to the furnace. Where it all broke down again, after looking so good for so long. His feet felt welded to the ground.

With an inhale of bottled air, he steeled himself and squeezed himself through the doors and into the quiet foundry.

Darkness swallowed him. Heat assailed him from all sides. For a moment, his only sensory observations were the taste of rubber and stale air on his tongue, and the rasp and hiss of his breaths, each faster than the last one. He fumbled for the wide-beam light on his hip, nearly dropping it before his thumb found the switch.

A dome of soft light pierced the dark. Talvek blinked away the initial wave of pain in his eyes, and piece by piece, the foundry took shape around him. Right next to his feet, the pool of steel-ooze still radiated carbonous heat on his face, and on his overall-covered skin, black and grey and its surface crazed by countless cracks. Talvek took a reading with his radiative temperature meter and radioed the results to the logging team.

‘Fifty above boiling. Edge of spill, gate, ladle-side.’

‘Received.’ Then, a different voice, one that Talvek recognized as the master himself, even over the interference. ‘Careful in there.’

He began edging his way around the spill, cautious to keep his balance and not to step on it. A drop of sweat slid down his back already. He wished the breather had a tube for drinking, like the firefighter’s equipment did.

As he moved on and his eyes adjusted to the gloom, he saw more of the hall. A tangle of metal lay tangled on the floor, by the right wall. It took a moment‌ to realize it was the staircase to the control chamber, or what remained of it. The heat must have melted the lowermost supports and softened the aluminum rivets joining the elements to each other, causing it to crash down under its own weight. He knew it must have happened well after the dump, but a shiver of apprehension went through him all the same.

Slowly, Talvek’s other senses began to work as he cented his way forward. The creak of his boots on the floor sounded utterly alien. _Burn residue coating the floor,_ he told himself and swallowed. The air smelled of baked clay and left a taste of metal in his throat. Both were faint, barely there, but enough to be more than products of imagination alone. The breather was not fully sealed against his face.

He was sweating profusely now, sucking in bottled air as his left side baked under the overall. His side, turned away from the melt, felt cold by comparison. Against all reason, it felt like life was slowly leaking out of him through it and into the cold dark facing him. It wasn’t cold, of course. The entire hall was as hot as a steamroom.

A drip to his left made him startle. Nothing moved in his bubble of light. Talvek waited for a moment, then was about to turn back just as the sound repeated itself. Water dropping. He angled the light upward. The struts under the secondary conveyor were beaded with droplets of water. Likewise, the girders, further up, glinting like glass as his beam swept over them. Water condensing. Maybe a pipe had burst somewhere, making the air as suffocatingly humid as it was. Even with the breather, he felt it on his forehead, and in his overall getting heavier by the tick.

Talvek walked sideways, hand outstretched to avoid bumping into a wall or an abandoned tool cabinet. He was getting dizzy already, and he could feel a brief tremor deep inside his right leg. The beads of water above transfixed his eyes. The ones closest to the pool shifted through colors as the light moved. As he watched, another silver drop of water detached from the closest one and dripped on the melt below. It made a hiss as it evaporated.

Satisfied at having discovered the cause of the sound, Talvek lowered the light and turned in one combined move, only to jump back with a yelp muffled under his breather. His flinching motion sent the beam of light astray, hiding whatever it was he had half-seen inside the darkness. Suddenly, Talvek felt horribly exposed. He turned the beam around, back and forth, no longer sure which way he had been facing. The breather no longer seemed capable of delivering the air he needed. He wanted to run, needed to run, but his feet wouldn’t work. The world spun around him.

Then his beam found it again. A disk of pure darkness, wider than he was tall by at least a span, hovering in the air where only empty space should be. The sheer unfamiliarity of it, the unexpectedness, had startled him so. It was the furnace, the mouth of the furnace obviously, but he had never seen it like that. It was upturned and dead like the rest of the site.

As Talvek's breathing slowed down, from a deafening rasp drowning everything underneath itself, into something like the aftermath of a good game of scrapball, his mind began to work again. The furnace turned from an object of wrongness into trade again. It wasn’t absolute black, just coated in a thin, uniform layer of char, almost pure matte. The light caught faintly where cracks spider-webbed over the glassed surface of the ceramic tiles lining the inner surface, but as a whole it looked serviceable. Unless the lining was more severely damaged further down—further in or sideways, in its current alignment—they could make it work again.

Yes, there was work to do, and his inspection here would make sure it got started in the right direction. He briefly described the cracking pattern on his radio. When he got back, he would sketch it on a slate or a piece of paper, to be shown to the engineers later. The exterior of the furnace looked fine, Talvek noted as he moved on his light, at least as far as he could see from his place. He wasn't so sure about the cradle—a truss lattice of leg-thick I-beams holding the million-ton structure aloft. The paint on them had cracked, forming a tessellation of thumb-sized flakes, their edges curled and blackened. The steel underneath should be fine, but if the welds were deemed unsafe, it would be weeks of work to rebuild or brace the supports. Talvek tutted.

But even that would be precision work—requiring ‌keen focus yet comparatively little physical exertion—compared to what clearing the pool of solid steel and slag would take. 

Talvek turned towards the grey lake again. And his legs gave out.

He barely heard the crack of his air bottles as they hit the concrete, barely felt the twinge in his neck as it whipped back. He slid down and sideways, ending halfway between lying on his back and lying on his side, propped against the air bottle.

He was done.

All strength fled from his limbs. His vision greyed out on the edges, for stars to spark in. Like Cooling’s, a thought gone almost before it had formed.

There was no way he could get up again.

He lay there, propped over the gas bottle, in a position that was bound to be awkward but that he no longer felt. His head hung, beads of sweat traveling upwards towards his forehead.

A few spans away, the spill continued baking the life out of him.

It felt more solid now. Seeming to stretch all the way towards the walls and the main gate. _That_ would be back-breaking labor. But not for him. He wouldn’t trudge there with the workers, lancing cuts into the melt for bars to pry it apart, and then heaving the smaller pieces onto awaiting carts or fetching a hoist for the larger ones. Sweat running down his back under protective overalls, the exterior world muffled and constrained by hearing protectors and goggled respirators. Staggering back to his place after his shift, or maybe he’d sleep on the foundry floor—it wouldn’t matter—after a few hours of dreamless sleep, it would just begin again.

It would take months, only for them to begin the actual work of making up for the lost production. Somewhere beyond or below his conscious thought, his hand fumbled for the light. The beam passed from the spill up into the conveyors and the feed chute, and down again, where the uppermost part of the ladle car could be seen on the opposite side of the concrete pier. Serving the same process but in mirrored roles, they fed a continuous stream of preheated scrap and ore into the furnace, and then tapped out the melt for further processing.

A droplet formed on the edge of his right eyebrow and fell onto the floor, dozens of spans away.

They would need to upgrade both systems, even before they could begin actual production. He had known they’d miss their levies even before he pulled that thrice-slagged lever—forever ago, it felt now—but it wasn’t until he spoke with the planner overseers that he learned of the new levies and how bad it really was. He had argued against it, voice raised and fists on the table as he leaned over, until the master himself had come and calmly asked him to see the numbers. Steel was needed to refill the stockpiles and underground warehouses, and Oerfil would deliver.

His right eye filled with sweat. It stung. He didn’t blink.

A fifth more. The thought made Talvek grimace under his breather. They could increase their output by a fifth, yes, but what they produced would be high in carbon and impurities. Barely better than pig iron, brittle and difficult to work. Caravaning it out would have been a disgrace to the entire foundry, from the master to the first ‘prentices sorting scrap on the yard, as recently as when Talvek had been a second apprentice. But the demand had exploded since then, and relaxed standards had been introduced as a response. Millions of tons of grade E were needed for mesh bastions and temporary bracing, and _somebody_ needed to produce them.

He could no longer feel his legs. Not even the ever-present pain in his knee.

It would be grueling, round-the-clock work to keep the furnace fed and the steel running out for casting and rolling. Nobody would be spared. Even the controllers in the chamber would have to maintain intense focus for their entire shift, hands on levers and dials, making constant adjustments to keep the redlined furnace stable. It would burn through the electrodes and the crew alike, especially at the beginning. But electrodes could be replaced, and within nine months, they would have the first of the new workforce trained to ease some of the load. Things would slowly return to normal.

But not for Talvek. He was finally out.

He would have liked to try farming.

The beam turned on the frozen lake again. The light painted the ridges and elevations a light glinting grey while leaving the crevices and depressions a deep black, like valleys in a winter sunrise when seen from high near the peaks. Half a dozen spans towards the upside wall, a crack bisected the edge of the lake, splitting into three almost immediately as it ran towards the center. It made Talvek think of the Three, the river-valleys that this culture had grown around and from in the ages past. The valleys—cracks—split into smaller valleys and gorges as Talvek’s eye followed them further upstream. There, three forks away from the Central, he could see Oerfil. The entire valley was dark, barely illuminated by the light reflecting from the mountainside, and cold. People moved there, but only a few; haggard survivors eking out a living among ruins, growing sustenance on collapsed roofs. The foundry stood empty, a full third of the keep’s wall collapsed on its weakened foundations. Inside, the furnace rested on its side on the floor, in a tangle of its collapsed cradle, left to rust where it lay. Brown blades of grass filled the cracks in the outer shell, frozen where they had died, ready to be replaced by fresh growth once the first rays of spring melted the ice.

A shape, eerily familiar, limped across the floor, circling around the spill and painstakingly climbing over collapsed girders and carefully treading on glassed concrete. Torn overalls hung from its malnourished frame, soiled and patched. Maybe it was Kador, from the way its shoulders were set, but no it couldn’t be. Malk then. It had built a hovel from pieces of scrap, leaning against the furnace. A piece of sheet metal propped into a crack in the furnace made its roof. Carefully, the shape lowered itself onto a knee and then crawled in, on its belly, all the way as Talvek watched, or saw, or was, or crawled. He was back in that house again, in the damp chill, back with those he had left, those he could not help, and around him that house grew and spread and extended, over the valley and beyond, to consume all he knew and ever would, endless and unyielding.

He was back, and this time there would be no late-night cargo train to stow him away.

The heat rushed back in. The pain. The radio was screaming static into the sweltering air, a noise that rose and fell and then slowly resolved into words. Requesting updates. Asking if he was alright.

Talvek made a croak, then a cough. His mouth was dry like a cured clay mold. A swallow washed away some of the taste of freshly reduced iron from his tongue. He began to laugh, a maniac’s chuckle muffled by his breather.

There was no way out for him.

A push with a foot brought him to his side. A movement, heavy as a shifting continent, and he was propped on his elbow. Then, on his hands and knees. Like a collapse in reverse, Talvek picked himself up from the floor. All the while he laughed, chuckled, snorts mixing with sobs and turning into fits of coughing and then a laugh again.

Eyes half-blind from sweat, and still shaking like he could collapse again at any point, Talvek brought his hand up to the radio on his chest.

# Interlude

Seven months, seven days, three hours, and four minutes after the accident, the foundry of Oerfil re-struck its arc. It happened without ceremony. The foundry master let out a relieved sigh, his shoulders sagging as he did, and nodded at the control crew. The furnace was theirs now.

Thousands of kilospans to the east, a wave of apprehension ran through the baker as the lights dimmed. His mind ran back to the past when the power had been out for weeks at a time. It felt like years, though barely a few months had passed. The baker had taken an enormous risk buying his new oven. Without power, he’d never be able to pay back the loan.

The light returned, and the baker resumed kneading the dough. A few yards, no, _spans_, to his left, the mill workers picked up their hushed discussion again. Their overalls were new. So were the beginnings of dark circles under their eyes.

# Epilogue

The control chamber shook and vibrated around Talvek. The low rumbling of the furnace and the busbars had an unhealthy intensity to it, and a rattle that hadn’t been there before underpinned it. It would have been deafening without his hearing protectors. Even with them cupping his ears, the sound grew to fill the entire world, shaking the pit of his stomach and even making his eyeballs buzz when the resonances shifted just right, but it was unlikely to cause lasting damage. At least, the new ones with the audio pickups had finally arrived yesterday. Perhaps today he could finish his shift without his voice worn into a rough whisper.

Talvek sat on a spare stool, spinning the ring on his fingers. It was iron, and rather crudely made, scuffed and oxidized. The anvil relief on the outer surface was worn almost smooth, but not as bad as the engraving on the inside. His finger could just make out it was there, but it was impossible to tell what it had once said.

It was Kador’s. It had been waiting for Talvek at his station, inside a small grey envelope with his name written on top, a half-finished bottle of fine liquor holding it in place. Inside had been the ring and a handwritten note from the overseer of the workforce. She had emptied Kador’s locker, presumably earlier that day. For ‌lack of known relatives, she’d thought Talvek should have it. Talvek wasn’t so sure about that, and closed his fist around the ring.

Less than five minutes had passed since his arrival, but he was already sweating. The windows of the control chamber radiated palpable heat, and the air tasted of carbon. For the floor crew, it was many times worse, Talvek knew. New, bigger air circulation fans were in the requisition pipeline, but Forge only knew how long they would be in the processing. Until then, they simply had to endure.

The apprentice arrived just moments before the bell, rushing into the chamber and nearly colliding with the evening shift operator collecting her slate, mug, and water bottle into a satchel. The hearing protectors still around his neck and his overall unzipped, it was obvious he’d been in a rush. Maybe he’d been sleeping late, or maybe there’d been a girl. Or maybe he’d been playing with his toys, Talvek thought, and only barely stopped himself from shaking his head. The kid looked far too young to have any business in the control chamber. Was he even a first apprentice yet? Talvek realized he had never asked. Well, it didn’t really matter. Apprentice or not, he was too inexperienced to be guiding an overworked furnace to the edge of boiling. Which was why Talvek was there with them. 

It was time. Talvek got up from his stool and immediately had to stifle a cough. The center of his chest burned. He leaned on his cane for support. Hazel, warm yellow-brown with darker spots. The doctor had told him that with enough time, his lungs might recover from the damage they had taken from the searing heat and reactive gases. Even in the worst case, they were unlikely to get any worse. Not much could be done about his knee, any more. She had told him so.

Talvek limped towards the panel, nodding his greeting to the kid. By now, he had gotten his protectors on and was fumbling with his zipper. His eyes were jumping from the panel to the windows and the eye-searing blueish glare cast by the furnace and reflected by the flue ducts, and back to the panel again. Behind him, the evening shift operator waved her farewells with a shout and disappeared into the stairwell, leaving Talvek with the boy.

He reached the panel, briefly catching his reflection on one of the dials. The skin on the left side of his face was wizened, cracks crisscrossing it like ravines in the badlands. The damage to the eye on that side remained invisible so far, the cloudiness in sight another legacy of the spill, though it was equally likely that he would develop cataracts in the years to come. He blinked in a subconscious, futile attempt to clear his vision, and took in the various dials and gauges on the panel.

The short walk had already left him feeling gassed. He took one deep breath and sank onto his seat, grateful as the old leather took his weight. Maybe he should look into glassmaking instead.

‘Hey! Pay attention to the preheat gauge.' He tapped at the offending panel for emphasis, and then leaned back in his chair as the kid began adjusting the controls, lost in concentration. His fist opened almost on its own, and his fingers began spinning the ring again.

‘The controls are still crazed. We’re slagged if the temps drop below three-fifty.’ He slid the ring onto his left index finger, the metal as if it had always belonged there, and turned to look at the youth.

‘Boy.’
